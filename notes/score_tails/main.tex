\date{October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
 Contrastive learning can be read as a methodology for learning a conditional
 distribution: a learned score tilts the marginal distribution of one modality,
 and the tilted measure is the model. Adopting this population-level viewpoint,
 developed by Baptista, Stuart and Tran, we ask which design choices in a
 contrastive forecaster survive in the limit of infinite data and flexible
 encoders: the loss (one-way, two-way, or joint), the statistical divergence
 that it minimizes, or the score. We first show that every loss variant, under
 every divergence, has the same population-level target, the log density ratio
 up to a conditioning-dependent constant; combined with the universal
 approximation theorems of Stuart and Wolf, the loss and the divergence drop
 out for flexible encoders. We then show that the score does not drop out, and
 that it matters precisely through its tail behaviour. A score that is bounded
 above defines a valid model for every data distribution, and the forecast
 obtained by reweighting a finite bank of observed futures converges to that
 model as the bank grows. A score that grows without bound in the future,
 such as the unnormalized inner product, admits no valid model on heavy-tailed
 data, even with ReLU or GELU encoders, and its bank forecast collapses onto a
 single future as the bank grows. This identifies the tail condition in existing
 non-compact universal approximation results as necessary rather than
 technical. Our main results concern the scores in between, which are bounded
 but level off along extreme futures, such as the normalized cosine or an
 inner product with a bounded future encoder. For regularly varying futures
 we show that the tail of the model is the tail of the marginal multiplied by
 a direction-dependent constant, the \emph{tail amplification} of the score.
 Such a forecast has the tail index of climatology for every past, whereas on
 heavy-tailed processes with a random scale the true conditional gains one
 unit of tail index per observed coordinate of the past. A finite bank hides
 the defect: the weight that the forecast places on a set of extreme futures
 grows linearly in the bank size $N$ until $N$ reaches the tail amplification
 $A$ and is constant thereafter, a $\min(N,A)$ law, and over the same range
 the expected excess energy score grows at least linearly in $N$. The
 KL-optimal bounded score is the clipped log density ratio, which saturates
 at its floor; a score saturates high because its architecture, not its
 bound, sends extreme futures to the top of its range. We identify a
 log-quadratic family that is well-specified on elliptical heavy-tailed data,
 and, since the Kullback--Leibler divergence charges a misplaced plateau for
 its mass while the energy distance charges for mass times distance, a
 training loss built from the energy score of the bank forecast itself.
 Numerical experiments on synthetic and real time series confirm the
 predictions.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Contrastive Learning, CLIP, Probabilistic
Forecasting, Importance Sampling, Heavy Tails, Model Misspecification

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Contrastive learning trains two encoders, one for each of two paired data
modalities, so that matched pairs receive a high score and mismatched pairs a
low one. In the forecasting setting considered here, the two modalities are
the past $u$ and the future $v$ of a time series. A recent line of work
\cite{baptista2025mathematical,stuart2026expressivity} views such algorithms
probabilistically: the learned score $s(u,v)$ defines a probability model for
$v$ given $u$, obtained by reweighting the marginal distribution of futures by
$e^{s}$, and the contrastive losses used in practice are statistical
divergences between this model and the data. This paper studies which design
choices in the resulting forecaster matter, and why.

In Subsection~\ref{ssec:BALR} we provide background and review related work.
Subsection~\ref{ssec:CAO} states our contributions and describes the
organization of the paper. Subsection~\ref{ssec:NOT} fixes notation.

\subsection{Background and Literature Review}
\label{ssec:BALR}

\paragraph{The probabilistic framework.}
Our starting point is the framework of Baptista, Stuart and Tran
\cite{baptista2025mathematical}. There, a score defines a tilting of the
product of the marginals, the CLIP loss and its one-way and joint variants are
identified as Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergences between the data and the
tilted model \cite[Thm.~3.2, Thm.~3.8, Rem.~3.12]{baptista2025mathematical},
generalizations to other divergences are formulated
\cite[Opt.~Probs.~3.4 and 3.6, Rem.~3.5]{baptista2025mathematical}, and a
forecast is obtained by reweighting a finite bank of observed futures
\cite[Eq.~(31)]{baptista2025mathematical}. For Gaussian data and linear
encoders every minimizer is available in closed form, and the choice of loss
and of score visibly changes the resulting forecast
\cite[Sec.~5]{baptista2025mathematical}.

\paragraph{Expressivity.}
Stuart and Wolf \cite{stuart2026expressivity} adopt the same population-level
view and study expressivity. They show that the joint KL divergence between
the data and the model bounds the conditional losses from above
\cite[Thms.~2.5, 2.6]{stuart2026expressivity}, that uniform approximation of
the log density ratio lifts to approximation of the measure in KL
\cite[Thms.~3.2, 3.3]{stuart2026expressivity}, and that two-tower CLIP, with
unnormalized or normalized encoders, is a universal approximator in KL on
compact domains \cite[Thms.~4.1, 4.2]{stuart2026expressivity}. Their
extension to non-compact domains truncates the approximating score outside a
compact set; the underlying estimate requires the approximant to be bounded
by a constant $B$ outside that set and carries an error term of order $e^{B}$
\cite[Prop.~A.3, Thm.~3.3]{stuart2026expressivity}. They work with KL
throughout and list other divergences, such as those built from the energy
score, as future work.

\paragraph{The optimal score.}
That the optimal InfoNCE critic is the pointwise mutual information plus a
function of the conditioning variable is standard
\cite{oord2018cpc,poole2019variational}. For the symmetric CLIP loss, Uesaka
et al.\ \cite{uesaka2025weighted} show that the optimal similarity is the
pointwise mutual information and argue that inner products of
finite-dimensional embeddings may fail to reach it. This is the closest
relative of the misspecification view taken in \Cref{sec:invariance}, but it
concerns a single loss rather than agreement or disagreement between
variants.

\paragraph{Reweighting candidates, and its failure.}
Sordoni et al.\ \cite{sordoni2021decomposed} note that resampling candidates
with softmax-normalized critic scores approximates the conditional
distribution, exactly as the pool of candidates grows; this is the bank
forecast studied here. Weight collapse of self-normalized importance sampling
is classical: in high dimension it is studied by Bengtsson, Bickel and Li
\cite{bengtsson2008curse}, heavy-tailed weights are diagnosed by
Pareto-smoothed importance sampling \cite{vehtari2024pareto}, and the
required sample size is of order $e^{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}$ by Chatterjee and
Diaconis \cite{chatterjee2018sample}. Likewise, an exponential tilt
$e^{\langle a, v\rangle}$ requires a finite moment generating function.
\Cref{sec:tails} examines how these properties govern the existence and
convergence of contrastive forecasters across different score architectures.

\paragraph{Bounded critics and normalization.}
In mutual-information estimation, Song and Ermon \cite{song2020limitations}
clip the critic to tame the variance of partition-function estimates. In
representation learning, normalized embeddings are standard because they
remove the scale degree of freedom that lets the softmax become arbitrarily
sharp \cite{chen2020simclr,wang2020understanding}, and negative squared
Euclidean distance logits are used in practice and match or exceed cosine
CLIP \cite{chou2024embedding}. To our knowledge none of these works ties
boundedness to the \emph{existence} of the model, or to the behaviour of a
reweighted bank.

\paragraph{Tails under exponential tilting, and training with scoring rules.}
How regular variation transforms under multiplication and tilting is
classical \cite{resnick2007heavy}; \Cref{ssec:saturation} applies it to the
tilted model and adds the finite-bank picture. Clipping an importance weight
or a critic is a standard variance-reduction device
\cite{ionides2008truncated,song2020limitations}; \Cref{prop:clip} shows that
the clipped log density ratio is also the population optimum of the
contrastive loss over bounded scores. Heavy-tailed, Cauchy-type similarity
kernels are used in neighbour embeddings and in their contrastive variants
\cite{maaten2008tsne,bohm2023unsupervised}, for geometric rather than
distributional reasons. Training generative forecasters by minimizing the
energy score is established practice
\cite{gneiting2007strictly,pacchiardi2024probabilistic,shen2024engression};
here the object being trained is a set of weights on observed futures rather
than a sampler.

\paragraph{Analog forecasting.}
Forecasting by weighting historical futures is the analog ensemble of
weather forecasting \cite{dellemonache2013analog} and kernel analog
forecasting \cite{zhao2016analog}. A contrastive bank forecast is an analog
forecast whose kernel, $e^{s(u,v)}$, is learned.

\subsection{Contribution and Paper Organization}
\label{ssec:CAO}

The primary contributions of this paper are a population-level account of
which design choices in a contrastive forecaster matter, together with
numerical evidence on synthetic and real time series. In detail, we make the
following contributions, structured to simultaneously describe the paper
organization:
\begin{enumerate}[start=1,label={(C\arabic*)}]
 \item \emph{Loss and divergence invariance} (\Cref{sec:invariance}).
       We show that the one-way, two-way and joint losses, under any
       divergence, vanish exactly when the score equals the log density ratio
       up to, respectively, a function of the past, a function of the future,
       or a constant. Consequently, all of them agree at the population level
       whenever the score family is well-specified. Combining this with the
       results of Stuart and Wolf, every KL-based loss variant is
       simultaneously driven to zero by flexible two-tower encoders on
       compact domains.
 \item \emph{Loss differences under restricted score families} (\Cref{ssec:gaussian}).
       In the Gaussian, linear setting of \cite{baptista2025mathematical} we
       show that each score family omits a specific quadratic component of the
       log density ratio, which characterizes the closed-form predictions of
       \cite{baptista2025mathematical} through score misspecification. We
       prove that linear $L^2$ scores cannot reach zero two-way or joint loss
       on any correlated Gaussian, and give the scalar optimum in closed form.
 \item \emph{Admissibility is governed by the tails of the score}
       (\Cref{ssec:admissibility}).
       A score bounded above yields a valid model for every data
       distribution. A score linear in the future yields no valid model on
       heavy-tailed data unless it ignores the past, and standard piecewise-linear
       or smooth positively homogeneous encoders (such as ReLU or GELU MLPs) do not
       remove this obstruction. Boundedness has a price: a bounded score can
       be exact only when the density ratio is bounded.
 \item \emph{Convergence and collapse of the bank forecast}
       (\Cref{ssec:bank}).
       We show that the bank forecast converges as the bank grows if and only
       if the score is admissible, that it collapses onto a single future
       when the scores over the bank are regularly varying, and that a
       two-sided bound guarantees a uniform effective sample size at the cost
       of capped sharpness. This shows that the tail condition in
       \cite[Prop.~A.3]{stuart2026expressivity} is necessary rather than a
       convenience of the proof.
 \item \emph{Saturating scores: tail amplification and exposure by the bank}
       (\Cref{ssec:saturation}).
       For regularly varying futures we determine the tail of the tilted
       model from the behaviour of the score along rays
       (\Cref{thm:tail}): the forecast has tail index $\nu+\beta$, where
       $\nu$ is the index of the marginal and $\beta$ the logarithmic decay
       rate of the score, and a score that levels off ($\beta=0$) transmits
       to the tail only a direction-dependent constant, its tail
       amplification. On elliptical heavy-tailed data the true conditional
       has index $\nu+d_u$, so the inner product, the normalized cosine and
       $L^2$ all have the wrong tail (\Cref{cor:trichotomy}), and we exhibit
       a log-quadratic family that is well-specified
       (\Cref{prop:logquad}). We prove a $\min(N,A)$ law for the weight that
       a bank of size $N$ places on extreme futures under a score with tail
       amplification $A$ (\Cref{thm:exposure}), and a matching lower bound on
       the expected excess energy score, which grows linearly in $N$ up to
       $N\approx A$ (\Cref{thm:energycost}). We show that the KL-optimal
       bounded score is the clipped log density ratio and hence saturates at
       its floor (\Cref{prop:clip}), so that saturating high is a property
       of the architecture, and that the Kullback--Leibler divergence
       charges a plateau for its mass while the energy distance charges for
       mass times distance (\Cref{prop:klvsenergy}), which motivates a
       training loss built from the bank forecast itself
       (\Cref{prop:bankenergy}).
 \item \emph{Numerical experiments} (\Cref{sec:experiments}).
       On synthetic Gaussian, heavy-tailed, warped and bimodal processes, and
       on real weather and financial data, we confirm the loss invariance for
       flexible encoders, the failure of unbounded scores on heavy tails, the
       $\min(N,A)$ law and the turnaround of saturating scores as the bank
       grows, the benefit of scores with a logarithmic tail, and the effect
       of training with the bank energy loss.
\end{enumerate}
\Cref{sec:conclusion} concludes and discusses open directions.
Proofs are collected in \Cref{app:proofs}.

\subsection{Notation}
\label{ssec:NOT}

For a measurable space we write $\Prob(\cdot)$ for the set of probability
measures on it. The past $u$ takes values in $\UU\subseteq\RR^{d_u}$, the
future $v$ in $\VV\subseteq\RR^{d_v}$, and $\XX\define\UU\times\VV$. The data
distribution is $\mu\in\Prob(\XX)$, with marginals $\mu_u$ and $\mu_v$ and
product of marginals $\muP\define\mu_u\otimes\mu_v$. Conditionals are written
$\mu_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$ and $\mu_{u\vert v}(\cdot\vert v)$. Euclidean
inner products and norms are $\IP{\cdot}{\cdot}$ and $\abs{\cdot}$. For
symmetric matrices, $A\preceq B$ denotes the Loewner order. $\delta_x$ is the
Dirac measure at $x$, and $\indicator_A$ the indicator of a set $A$.

\section{Probabilistic Formulation}
\label{sec:setting}
% ===========================================================================

In this section we specify the data model (\Cref{ssec:problem}), the model
defined by a score (\Cref{ssec:model}), the loss functions
(\Cref{ssec:losses}), and the finite-sample forecast that is used in
practice (\Cref{ssec:bankdef}). The presentation follows
\cite{baptista2025mathematical,stuart2026expressivity}.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{ssec:problem}

\begin{assumption}[Data Distribution]
 \label{ass:data}
 The data generating measure $\mu\in\Prob(\XX)$ satisfies the following.
 \begin{enumerate}[start=1,label={(A\arabic*)}]
  \item \label{ass:marginals}\emph{Marginal distributions.}
        The marginals $\mu_u\in\Prob(\UU)$ and $\mu_v\in\Prob(\VV)$ are
        absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure.
  \item \label{ass:joint}\emph{Joint distribution.}
        The measures $\mu$ and $\muP$ are mutually absolutely continuous, with
        Radon--Nikodym derivative
        \begin{equation}
         r(u,v)\define\frac{\Dd\mu}{\Dd\muP}(u,v)
         =\frac{p(v\vert u)}{p(v)}.
         \label{eq:ratio}
        \end{equation}
  \item \label{ass:MI}\emph{Finite mutual information.}
        $\log r\in L^1(\XX,\mu)$, so that
        $I(u;v)\define\E_{\mu}\log r<\infty$.
 \end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

The function $\log r$ is the pointwise mutual information: it measures how
much more likely the future $v$ is after observing the past $u$ than it is a
priori. It is the tilting function of \cite{stuart2026expressivity}, and it
is the object that every loss studied below aims to recover.

\subsection{The Model Defined by a Score}
\label{ssec:model}

\begin{definition}[Score and Tilted Model]
 \label{def:model}
 A \emph{score} is a measurable function $s:\XX\to\RR$. It defines the
 joint and conditional measures
 \begin{equation}
  \nu^{s}(\Dd u,\Dd v)\define\frac{e^{s(u,v)}}{Z}\,\muP(\Dd u,\Dd v),
  \qquad
  \nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\Dd v\vert u)\define\frac{e^{s(u,v)}}{Z(u)}\,\mu_v(\Dd v),
  \label{eq:model}
 \end{equation}
 with normalizing constants
 \begin{equation}
  Z\define\int_{\XX}e^{s}\,\Dd\muP,\qquad
  Z(u)\define\int_{\VV}e^{s(u,v)}\,\mu_v(\Dd v),\qquad
  Z'(v)\define\int_{\UU}e^{s(u,v)}\,\mu_u(\Dd u),
  \label{eq:normalizers}
 \end{equation}
 and $\nu^{s}_{u\vert v}$ defined analogously with $Z'(v)$
 \cite[Eqs.~(13)--(16)]{baptista2025mathematical}. When $Z<\infty$, the
 conditional of $\nu^{s}$ given $u$ is $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}$.
\end{definition}

Intuitively, the forecast for a past $u$ takes the marginal distribution of
futures and reweights each trajectory by $e^{s(u,v)}$. The construction requires
the normalizing constants to be finite, which motivates the following
definition; it is central to \Cref{sec:tails}.

\begin{definition}[Admissible Score]
 \label{def:admissible}
 A score $s$ is \emph{admissible for the forecasting direction} if
 $Z(u)<\infty$ for $\mu_u$-almost every $u$, and \emph{admissible} if, in
 addition, $Z'(v)<\infty$ for $\mu_v$-almost every $v$ and $Z<\infty$. If
 a normalizing constant is infinite, the corresponding model does not exist,
 and we assign the value $+\infty$ to every loss that involves it.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Scores]
 \label{ex:scores}
 Let $f:\UU\to\RR^{d_z}$ and $g:\VV\to\RR^{d_z}$ be encoders of the past and
 the future, and let $\kappa>0$ be a learnable scale (the inverse
 temperature, $\kappa=1/\tau$ in \cite{stuart2026expressivity}). We consider
 \begin{equation}
  \sdot=\kappa\IP{f(u)}{g(v)},\qquad
  \sncos=\kappa\IP{\frac{f(u)}{\abs{f(u)}}}{\frac{g(v)}{\abs{g(v)}}},\qquad
  \sltwo=-\frac{\kappa}{2}\abs{f(u)-g(v)}^{2}.
  \label{eq:scores}
 \end{equation}
 The unnormalized inner product $\sdot$ is the score of
 \cite[Thm.~4.1]{stuart2026expressivity}, the normalized cosine $\sncos$
 (defined for non-vanishing encoders) is that of CLIP \cite{radford2021clip}
 and of \cite[Thm.~4.2]{stuart2026expressivity}, and $\sltwo$ is the $L^2$
 score of \cite{baptista2025mathematical}. Note that $\sltwo\le0$ and
 $\abs{\sncos}\le\kappa$, whereas $\sdot$ is unbounded whenever $g$ is.
\end{example}

\subsection{Population-Level Loss Functions}
\label{ssec:losses}

\begin{definition}[Divergence]
 \label{def:divergence}
 A \emph{divergence} is a map $D:\Prob\times\Prob\to[0,\infty]$ with
 $D(p,q)=0$ if and only if $p=q$.
\end{definition}

The KL divergence is a divergence. So is the energy distance, on
distributions with a finite first moment,
\begin{equation}
 \DE(p,q)\define2\,\E\abs{X-Y}-\E\abs{X-X'}-\E\abs{Y-Y'},
 \qquad X,X'\sim p,\;Y,Y'\sim q\text{ independent},
 \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
as is the maximum mean discrepancy with a characteristic kernel. The energy
distance is also our evaluation metric: the expected energy score
\cite{gneiting2007strictly} of a forecast $q$, minus that of the truth $p$,
equals $\tfrac12\DE(p,q)$.

\begin{definition}[Contrastive Losses]
 \label{def:losses}
 For a divergence $D$, weights $\lambda_u,\lambda_v>0$ and a score $s$,
 define the \emph{one-way}, \emph{reverse one-way}, \emph{two-way} and
 \emph{joint} losses
 \begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
   \Jvu^{D}(s)    & \define\E_{u\sim\mu_u}\,D\bigl(\mu_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u),\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)\bigr), &
   \Juv^{D}(s)    & \define\E_{v\sim\mu_v}\,D\bigl(\mu_{u\vert v}(\cdot\vert v),\nu^{s}_{u\vert v}(\cdot\vert v)\bigr),   \\
   \Jtwo^{D}(s)   & \define\lambda_u\Jvu^{D}(s)+\lambda_v\Juv^{D}(s),                                                    &
   \Jjoint^{D}(s) & \define D\bigl(\mu,\nu^{s}\bigr).
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:losses}
 \end{equation}
 When $D=D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ we drop the superscript.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Relation to CLIP and InfoNCE]
 \label{rem:clip}
 With $D=D_{\mathrm{KL}}$, expanding \eqref{eq:losses} with \eqref{eq:model}
 gives $\Jvu(s)=I(u;v)-\E_{\mu}[s]+\E_{\mu_u}[\log Z(u)]$. Replacing the
 measures by empirical measures yields the InfoNCE loss for $\Jvu$, the CLIP
 loss for $\Jtwo$ with $\lambda_u=\lambda_v=\tfrac12$, and a single softmax
 over all pairs for $\Jjoint$
 \cite[Thm.~3.2, Thm.~3.8, Rem.~3.12]{baptista2025mathematical}; see also
 \cite[Sec.~2.2]{stuart2026expressivity}. For general $D$ the losses
 correspond to \cite[Opt.~Probs.~3.4 and 3.6]{baptista2025mathematical}; they
 can be trained from samples alone when $D$ is the KL divergence, an energy
 distance, or a maximum mean discrepancy
 \cite[Rem.~3.5]{baptista2025mathematical}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Bank Forecast}
\label{ssec:bankdef}

The model \eqref{eq:model} involves the full marginal $\mu_v$. In practice it
is replaced by a finite collection of observed futures.

\begin{dataAssumption}
 \label{dass:bank}
 We have access to a \emph{bank} $v_1,\ldots,v_N\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mu_v$
 of futures, independent of the data used to train the score.
\end{dataAssumption}

Following \cite[Eq.~(31)]{baptista2025mathematical}, the \emph{bank forecast}
for a past $u$ is
\begin{equation}
 \nu^{N}_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)\define\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_j\,\delta_{v_j},\qquad
 w_j\define\frac{e^{s(u,v_j)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{s(u,v_k)}},\qquad
 \ESS\define\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_j^{2}\Bigr)^{-1}.
 \label{eq:bank}
\end{equation}
This is self-normalized importance sampling with proposal $\mu_v$ and target
$\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}$. The effective sample size $\ESS\in[1,N]$ counts how
many bank futures the forecast effectively uses; $\ESS\approx1$ means that
the forecast has collapsed onto a single future.

\section{Loss and Divergence Invariance}
\label{sec:invariance}
% ===========================================================================

In this section we show that every loss variant, under every divergence,
aims at the same object, the log density ratio. The variants differ only in
which part of it they leave free, and the divergences differ only in how they
measure a miss. Differences between them are therefore a symptom of a score
family that cannot represent $\log r$.

\begin{proposition}[Common Target]
 \label{prop:target}
 Let $\mu$ satisfy \Cref{ass:data}, let $D$ be any divergence, and let $s$ be
 an admissible score. Then
 \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $\Jvu^{D}(s)=0$ if and only if $s(u,v)=\log r(u,v)+c(u)$ for some
        function $c$;
  \item $\Juv^{D}(s)=0$ if and only if $s(u,v)=\log r(u,v)+c'(v)$ for some
        function $c'$;
  \item $\Jtwo^{D}(s)=0$ if and only if $\Jjoint^{D}(s)=0$ if and only if
        $s=\log r+\mathrm{const}$.
 \end{enumerate}
 All identities hold $\muP$-almost everywhere. The right-hand sides do not
 involve $D$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:target}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Well-Specified Family]
 \label{def:wellspecified}
 A family $\mathcal{S}$ of scores is \emph{well-specified} for a loss if that
 loss attains the value $0$ on $\mathcal{S}$. By \Cref{prop:target}, this means
 that $\mathcal{S}$ contains some $\log r+c(u)$ for the one-way loss, and
 $\log r+\mathrm{const}$ for the two-way and joint losses, whatever the
 divergence.
\end{definition}

\begin{corollary}[Agreement]
 \label{cor:agreement}
 If $\mathcal{S}$ contains $\log r+\mathrm{const}$, then for every divergence
 $D$ the one-way, two-way (for any $\lambda_u,\lambda_v>0$) and joint losses
 all have minimizers whose forecast is exactly the true conditional,
 $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}=\mu_{v\vert u}$. Neither the loss variant nor the
 divergence can be distinguished at the population level.
\end{corollary}

The converse fails: \Cref{ssec:gaussian} contains a family that cannot
represent $\log r$, yet for which the one-way and two-way losses agree.

\subsection{Flexible Encoders}
\label{ssec:flexible}

\Cref{cor:agreement} requires the family to contain $\log r$ exactly. For
flexible encoders, the results of Stuart and Wolf provide the approximate
analogue for the KL divergence.

\begin{theorem}[Approximate Agreement, KL]
 \label{thm:approxagreement}
 Let $\mu$ satisfy \Cref{ass:data} with $\UU$ and $\VV$ compact, and let
 the encoders be drawn from a parametric family satisfying the
 $C^0$-universal approximation assumption of
 \cite[Sec.~3]{stuart2026expressivity}. Then for every $\epsilon>0$
 there exist a latent dimension $d_z$ and encoders $f,g$ such that
 $s=\sdot$ satisfies
 \begin{equation*}
  \Jjoint(s)<\epsilon,\qquad\Jvu(s)<\epsilon,\qquad\Juv(s)<\epsilon,
  \qquad\Jtwo(s)<(\lambda_u+\lambda_v)\,\epsilon.
 \end{equation*}
 The same holds for $s=\sncos$, with $\kappa$ chosen large enough.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:approxagreement}.
\end{proof}

A single flexible score therefore makes every KL-based loss variant small
simultaneously. This is the first half of our account: once the encoders
are flexible, the loss variant drops out. The statement is restricted to
compact domains, on which continuous encoders produce bounded scores;
\Cref{sec:tails} studies what happens when that boundedness is lost.

\Cref{thm:approxagreement} does not transfer to other divergences, because
closeness in KL does not imply closeness in other divergences. The simplest
case is a forecast that is correct except for a small amount of mass moved far
away.

\begin{proposition}[KL Is Blind to How Far Misplaced Mass Goes]
 \label{prop:klblind}
 Let $p$ be a distribution on $\RR^{d}$ with a finite mean and no atom at
 $a\in\RR^d$, and let $q_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)p+\varepsilon\delta_a$.
 Then
 \begin{equation*}
  \KL{p}{q_\varepsilon}\le-\log(1-\varepsilon)\quad\text{for every }a,
 \end{equation*}
 whereas
 \begin{equation*}
  \tfrac12\DE(p,q_\varepsilon)
  =\tfrac12\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(2\,\E\abs{X-a}-\E\abs{X-X'}\bigr)
  \xrightarrow[\abs{a}\to\infty]{}\infty.
 \end{equation*}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:klblind}.
\end{proof}

Moving a fraction $\varepsilon$ of the forecast to an arbitrarily extreme
future costs at most about $\varepsilon$ in KL, while its cost in energy
score grows linearly with the distance. A model trained with KL therefore has
almost no incentive to keep mass out of the far tails, whereas an evaluation
by energy score penalizes exactly that. The divergences thus agree exactly at
the optimum (\Cref{cor:agreement}) and approximately in the bulk
(\Cref{thm:approxagreement}), but an approximation that is good in one may be
poor in another in the tails. Training with the energy distance itself, as
\cite[Rem.~3.5]{baptista2025mathematical} allows, would penalize tail
over-weighting directly.

\subsection{Rigid Families: the Gaussian Case}
\label{ssec:gaussian}

When the family is not flexible, the loss variants come apart. The Gaussian,
linear setting of \cite[Sec.~5]{baptista2025mathematical} makes this
explicit. There, every minimizer is available in closed form, and four
predictions are relevant here:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(P\arabic*),ref=P\arabic*]
 \item \label{P1} the inner-product score gets the mean of the forecast right
       but its spread wrong, which remains at the unconditional level
       \cite[Cor.~5.2]{baptista2025mathematical};
 \item \label{P2} the one-way $L^2$ score gets both mean and spread right
       \cite[Thm.~5.3, Cor.~5.4]{baptista2025mathematical};
 \item \label{P3} for the inner-product score, the one-way and two-way losses
       give the same answer \cite[Thm.~5.1]{baptista2025mathematical};
 \item \label{P4} the joint loss shrinks the forecast towards the mean and is
       a worse forecaster than the one-way loss
       \cite[Thm.~5.6, Cor.~5.7]{baptista2025mathematical}.
\end{enumerate}
Each of these phenomena can be understood directly through score
misspecification.

Let $\mu$ be a centred Gaussian with covariance blocks $C_{uu},C_{uv},C_{vv}$,
joint covariance $C$, joint precision $P=C^{-1}$, and
$C_0\define\mathrm{blockdiag}(C_{uu},C_{vv})$, the covariance of $\muP$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \log r(u,v)=-\tfrac12u^{\top}(P_{uu}-C_{uu}^{-1})u\;-\;u^{\top}P_{uv}v
 \;-\;\tfrac12v^{\top}(P_{vv}-C_{vv}^{-1})v+\mathrm{const}.
 \label{eq:gaussianlogr}
\end{equation}
There are three ingredients: a past--future interaction, which sets the mean
of the forecast; a future-only term, which sets its spread; and a past-only
term. With linear encoders every score is of the form
$s=-\tfrac12u^{\top}Ru+u^{\top}Av-\tfrac12v^{\top}Sv$, and the families
differ in which ingredients they can set (\Cref{tab:families}).

\Cref{tab:families} recovers the predictions. For \ref{P1}: the inner
product has no future-only term, so the covariance of its forecast is
$C_{vv}$ whatever $A$ is. For \ref{P4}: the joint loss also needs the
past-only term, which the inner product lacks; moreover, the joint normalizer
is finite only if $\norm{C_{uu}^{1/2}AC_{vv}^{1/2}}<1$, which is the origin of
the shrinkage function of \cite{baptista2025mathematical}. For \ref{P2}: the
one-way $L^2$ family is well-specified, because $S\succeq0$ can match the
future-only term. The two-way and joint $L^2$ losses are not.

\begin{proposition}[Linear $L^2$ Is Too Rigid for the Two-Way and Joint Losses]
 \label{prop:l2rigid}
 Under linear $L^2$ scores, every model joint covariance satisfies
 $(C_0^{-1}+Q)^{-1}\preceq C_0$, with $Q=[G,-H]^{\top}[G,-H]\succeq0$. The
 data satisfy $C\not\preceq C_0$ whenever $C_{uv}\neq0$. Hence the two-way
 and joint losses cannot reach zero.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:l2rigid}.
\end{proof}

Geometrically, the quadratic correction $Q$ of a linear $L^2$ score is positive
semidefinite, so the model can only shrink the covariance of the product of
marginals: it couples past and future only by shrinking both marginals. A
correlated Gaussian couples them while keeping its marginals fixed, which would
require an indefinite correction.

\begin{proposition}[Scalar Closed Form]
 \label{prop:scalar}
 For unit-variance scalars with correlation $\rho\in(0,1)$, the joint-$L^2$
 optimum forecasts
 $\mathcal{N}\bigl(\tfrac{\rho}{2-\rho}u,\tfrac{2(1-\rho)}{2-\rho}\bigr)$,
 against the truth $\mathcal{N}(\rho u,1-\rho^{2})$. The mean is shrunk and the
 forecast is over-confident. The fraction of the mutual information $I(u;v)$
 that it loses tends to $3/8$ as $\rho\to0$, and to $0$ as $\rho\to1$
 (for the symmetric two-way-$L^2$ optimum, the corresponding fraction tends
 to $1/3$ as $\rho\to0$ and $0$ as $\rho\to1$).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:scalar}.
\end{proof}

% ===========================================================================
\section{Admissibility and the Tails of the Score}
\label{sec:tails}
% ===========================================================================

Flexibility concerns representing $\log r$ where the data are. It says
nothing about how the score behaves far from the data, and that is where the
choice of score continues to matter. The non-compact universal approximation
result of \cite[Thm.~3.3]{stuart2026expressivity} rests on an estimate
\cite[Prop.~A.3]{stuart2026expressivity} which requires the approximating
score to satisfy $\abs{s}\le B$ outside a compact set $\KK$, and whose error
contains a term of order $e^{B}$; the theorem then enforces the bound by
truncating the approximant to zero outside $\KK$. For a two-tower score and
$\KK=\KK_u\times\KK_v$, truncation corresponds to encoders that vanish
outside compact sets, since
$\indicator_{\KK}\IP{f}{g}=\IP{\indicator_{\KK_u}f}{\indicator_{\KK_v}g}$.
Standard neural encoders do not vanish. In this section we show that the tail
condition is not a convenience of the proof: without it the model need not
exist, and the bank forecast collapses.

\subsection{Admissibility}
\label{ssec:admissibility}

\begin{proposition}[Bounded Above Implies Admissible]
 \label{prop:bounded}
 If $s\le c$, then $Z(u)\le e^{c}$, $Z'(v)\le e^{c}$ and $Z\le e^{c}$ for
 every data distribution $\mu$. In particular, $\sltwo$ (with $c=0$) and
 $\sncos$ (with $c=\kappa$) are admissible for every $\mu$, with any
 encoders.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:bounded}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[A Score Linear in the Future Needs Light Tails]
 \label{prop:linear}
 Let $s(u,v)=\IP{a(u)}{Hv}+b(u)$. Then $Z(u)=e^{b(u)}M_{\mu_v}(H^{\top}a(u))$,
 where $M_{\mu_v}$ is the moment generating function of $\mu_v$. If $\mu_v$
 is a multivariate Student-$t$ distribution, with any number of degrees of
 freedom, then $M_{\mu_v}(w)=\infty$ for every $w\neq0$. Hence the linear
 inner-product score is admissible for the forecasting direction only if
 $H^{\top}a(u)=0$ for $\mu_u$-almost every $u$, that is, only if it ignores
 the past.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:linear}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[ReLU and GELU Encoders Do Not Restore Admissibility]
 \label{prop:relu}
 Let $s=\sdot$ where $g$ is a multilayer perceptron (with or without linear
 skip connections) whose activation $\sigma$ satisfies
 $\sup_{x\in\RR}\abs{\sigma(x)-\mathrm{ReLU}(x)}<\infty$ (such as ReLU or
 GELU), and let $g_\infty$ denote the corresponding network with $\sigma$
 replaced by $\mathrm{ReLU}$ and all biases removed. If
 $\IP{f(u)}{g_\infty(v_0)}>0$ for some direction $v_0$, and $\mu_v$ is an
 elliptical Student-$t$ distribution, then $Z(u)=\infty$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:relu}.
\end{proof}

The inner-product score is therefore admissible at $u$ only if it does not
grow in any direction of the future, and nothing in training enforces this.
A bounded output layer on $g$, such as $\tanh$, makes the score admissible by
\Cref{prop:bounded} without normalizing anything; \Cref{ssec:banksweep}
shows why this alone is still problematic if the score saturates monotonically
along rays.

Boundedness is not free. Combining \Cref{prop:target} with an upper bound on
the score constrains the density ratios that the family can represent
exactly.

\begin{proposition}[Bounded Scores Represent Only Bounded Ratios]
 \label{prop:price}
 Let $s\le c$ be admissible and let $D$ be any divergence.
 \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item If $\Jvu^{D}(s)=0$, then for $\mu_u$-almost every $u$,
        $r(u,\cdot)\le e^{c}/Z(u)$ holds $\mu_v$-almost everywhere.
  \item If $\Jjoint^{D}(s)=0$, then $r\le e^{c}/Z$ holds $\muP$-almost
        everywhere.
  \item If $\abs{s}\le\kappa$ and $\Jjoint^{D}(s)=0$, then
        $e^{-2\kappa}\le r\le e^{2\kappa}$ holds $\muP$-almost everywhere.
 \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:price}.
\end{proof}

For a correlated Gaussian, $r$ is unbounded on $\XX$, so no score bounded
above, however flexible, is well-specified for the joint or two-way loss;
\Cref{prop:l2rigid} is a special case. This does not contradict
\Cref{thm:approxagreement}: the approximants constructed in
\cite[Thm.~3.3]{stuart2026expressivity} are themselves bounded, being
continuous on $\KK$ and zero outside it. Boundedness thus costs exactness but
not approximability in KL. For the normalized cosine, part (iii) is the
counterpart of the observation in \cite[Thm.~4.2]{stuart2026expressivity}
that universality requires the temperature $1/\kappa$ to tend to zero.

\subsection{Convergence and Collapse of the Bank Forecast}
\label{ssec:bank}

Admissibility is not a technicality: it is exactly the condition under which
the bank forecast \eqref{eq:bank} works.

\begin{theorem}[Admissibility Is Equivalent to Bank Convergence]
 \label{thm:bank}
 Fix $u$ and let the bank satisfy \Cref{dass:bank}. If $Z(u)<\infty$, then
 $\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}\to\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}$ weakly, almost surely, as
 $N\to\infty$, and
 $\ESS/N\to1/\E_{\mu_v}\bigl[(\Dd\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}/\Dd\mu_v)^{2}\bigr]$ when
 this moment is finite. If $Z(u)=\infty$, then the empirical normalizer
 $\frac1N\sum_{j}e^{s(u,v_j)}$ diverges and there is no limit to converge
 to.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:bank}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Collapse]
 \label{prop:collapse}
 If the scores $X_j=s(u,v_j)$ of the bank futures have a regularly varying
 right tail, $\mathbb{P}(X>x)=x^{-\alpha}L(x)$ with $L$ slowly varying, then
 $\max_j w_j\to1$ in probability as $N\to\infty$. This covers the linear
 inner-product score on Student-$t$ futures, with $\alpha$ equal to the
 degrees of freedom.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:collapse}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Bounded on Both Sides: Guaranteed Bank, Capped Sharpness]
 \label{prop:twosided}
 If $\abs{s}\le\kappa$, then
 $e^{-2\kappa}\le\Dd\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}/\Dd\mu_v\le e^{2\kappa}$. Hence
 $\KL{\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}}{\mu_v}\le2\kappa$ and, in the limit $N\to\infty$,
 $\ESS/N\ge e^{-2\kappa}$, uniformly in $u$. The price is that no forecast can
 be more than $e^{2\kappa}$ times as concentrated as the marginal
 $\mu_v$, or carry more than $2\kappa$ nats of information.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:twosided}.
\end{proof}

The two regimes for bank convergence are therefore sharp. With a bounded score
the bank forecast converges to the tilted model as the bank grows. With an
unbounded score on heavy-tailed data it collapses onto the single most extreme
future, and a larger bank only supplies more extreme futures to collapse onto.
\Cref{tab:fixedscore} checks this for fixed scores, with no training, and
\Cref{tab:hierarchy} summarizes the hierarchy.

\begin{remark}[Why Training Does Not Avoid Inadmissibility]
 \label{rem:training}
 At the population level an inadmissible parameter has infinite loss, so an
 exact minimizer would avoid it. Training, however, uses the empirical loss,
 which is always finite: a batch of $256$ futures almost never contains the
 extreme futures that make $Z(u)$ infinite. Training can therefore settle on
 an informative parameter whose score grows in the future, and the problem
 appears only when the forecast meets a large bank. For the linear inner
 product on Student-$t$ data, \Cref{prop:linear} shows that every informative
 parameter is of this kind. \Cref{prop:klblind} gives a second reason: even
 when the model exists, KL training barely charges for misplaced tail mass.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Saturating Scores: Tail Amplification and Exposure by the Bank}
\label{ssec:saturation}

Admissibility separates the scores whose bank forecast converges from those
whose bank forecast collapses. It does not say that the limit is a good
forecast of extreme futures. In this subsection we show that the tail of the
tilted model is determined by the behaviour of the score along rays, that a
score which levels off along rays hands every past the tail of the marginal,
and that a finite bank hides this defect until its size reaches the height at
which the score levels off. Throughout, $S\define\{\theta\in\RR^{d_v}:\abs{\theta}=1\}$
denotes the unit sphere and $\bar F(t)\define\mu_v(\abs{v}>t)$ the marginal
tail.

\begin{assumption}[Regularly Varying Futures]
 \label{ass:rv}
 There exist an index $\nu>0$ and a probability measure $\Lambda$ on $S$, the
 \emph{angular measure}, such that for every $x\ge1$ and every Borel set
 $B\subseteq S$ with $\Lambda(\partial B)=0$,
 \begin{equation}
  \lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mu_v\bigl(\abs{v}>tx,\;v/\abs{v}\in B\bigr)}{\bar F(t)}
  =x^{-\nu}\,\Lambda(B).
  \label{eq:rv}
 \end{equation}
\end{assumption}

Equivalently, conditionally on $\abs{v}>t$ the pair $(\abs{v}/t,\,v/\abs{v})$
converges in distribution, as $t\to\infty$, to a pair $(R,\Theta)$ of
independent variables with $\mathbb{P}(R>x)=x^{-\nu}$ and $\Theta\sim\Lambda$
\cite{resnick2007heavy}. The elliptical Student-$t$ distribution with $\nu$
degrees of freedom satisfies \Cref{ass:rv} with index $\nu$ and an angular
measure that has a positive density on $S$.

\begin{definition}[Ray Profile and Tail Amplification]
 \label{def:profile}
 Fix a past $u$ with $Z(u)<\infty$ and a number $\beta\ge0$. The score
 $s(u,\cdot)$ has \emph{log-decay rate} $\beta$ and \emph{ray profile}
 $\ell(u,\cdot):S\to[-\infty,\infty)$ if
 \begin{enumerate}[label=(R\arabic*)]
  \item \label{R1} $\sup_{v\in\VV}\bigl[s(u,v)+\beta\log(1+\abs{v})\bigr]<\infty$, and
  \item \label{R2} for $\Lambda$-almost every $\theta\in S$,
        $s(u,t\theta')+\beta\log t\to\ell(u,\theta)$ whenever $t\to\infty$
        and $\theta'\to\theta$.
 \end{enumerate}
 The \emph{tail amplification} of the score at $u$ is
 \begin{equation}
  A(u,\theta)\define\frac{e^{\ell(u,\theta)}}{Z(u)},\qquad
  \bar A(u)\define\int_{S}A(u,\theta)\,\Lambda(\Dd\theta).
  \label{eq:amplification}
 \end{equation}
 We call the score \emph{saturating} at $u$ if it has log-decay rate $\beta=0$
 with $\bar A(u)>0$, and \emph{coercive} at $u$ if, for every $\beta\ge0$, it
 has log-decay rate $\beta$ with ray profile identically $-\infty$.
\end{definition}

For $\beta=0$ the amplification $A(u,\theta)$ is the limit along the ray
through $\theta$ of the tilting factor $\Dd\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}/\Dd\mu_v=e^{s}/Z(u)$,
so $A(u,\theta)>1$ means that the model gives extreme futures in direction
$\theta$ more weight than they carry under the marginal. This is the precise
sense in which a score can ``saturate high'': its limit along a ray lies above
$\log Z(u)$.

\begin{example}[Ray Profiles of Common Scores]
 \label{ex:profiles}
 Let $g$ be linear, or a multilayer perceptron as in \Cref{prop:relu}, and let
 $g_\infty$ be its positively homogeneous part, so that
 $\sup_v\abs{g(v)-g_\infty(v)}<\infty$ and $g_\infty$ is continuous. Assume
 that $\Lambda$ gives no mass to the directions $\theta$ in which some
 coordinate of $g_\infty(\theta)$ vanishes. Then, for every $u$ with $f(u)\ne0$:
 \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item the inner product with a bounded future encoder,
        $s=\kappa\IP{f(u)}{\tanh g(v)}$, has $\beta=0$ and
        $\ell(u,\theta)=\kappa\IP{f(u)}{\operatorname{sign}g_\infty(\theta)}$;
  \item the normalized cosine $\sncos$ has $\beta=0$ and
        $\ell(u,\theta)=\kappa\cos\angle\bigl(f(u),g_\infty(\theta)\bigr)$;
  \item the $L^2$ score $\sltwo$ is coercive whenever
        $\inf_{\theta\in S}\abs{g_\infty(\theta)}>0$;
  \item under the same condition, the \emph{Student-$t$ kernel score}
        \begin{equation}
         s_{t,b}(u,v)\define-\frac{b}{2}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{\kappa}{b}\abs{f(u)-g(v)}^{2}\Bigr),\qquad b>0,
         \label{eq:tkernel}
        \end{equation}
        which tends to $\sltwo$ as $b\to\infty$, has $\beta=b$ and
        $\ell(u,\theta)=-\tfrac{b}{2}\log\bigl(\kappa\abs{g_\infty(\theta)}^{2}/b\bigr)$;
  \item the inner product $\sdot$ violates \ref{R1} for every $\beta$ as soon
        as $\IP{f(u)}{g_\infty(\theta)}>0$ for some $\theta$ in the support of
        $\Lambda$, which is the situation of \Cref{prop:relu}.
 \end{enumerate}
 Scores (a) and (b) are saturating. They are also bounded on both sides, so
 \Cref{prop:twosided} applies to them; the theorem below replaces its
 worst-case constant $e^{-2\kappa}$ by the exact one.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Tail of the Tilted Model]
 \label{thm:tail}
 Let $\mu_v$ satisfy \Cref{ass:rv} and let $s(u,\cdot)$ have log-decay rate
 $\beta\ge0$ and ray profile $\ell(u,\cdot)$. Then for every Borel set
 $B\subseteq S$ with $\Lambda(\partial B)=0$,
 \begin{equation}
  \lim_{t\to\infty}
  \frac{\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}\bigl(\abs{v}>t,\;v/\abs{v}\in B\,\big\vert\,u\bigr)}{t^{-\beta}\,\bar F(t)}
  =\frac{\nu}{\nu+\beta}\int_{B}A(u,\theta)\,\Lambda(\Dd\theta).
  \label{eq:tail}
 \end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:tail}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Tail Index and Radial Blindness]
 \label{cor:tailindex}
 In the setting of \Cref{thm:tail}:
 \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item If $\bar A(u)>0$, the forecast $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$ is
        regularly varying with index $\nu+\beta$ and angular measure
        $A(u,\theta)\Lambda(\Dd\theta)/\bar A(u)$.
  \item If the score is saturating at $u$, then
        $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\abs{v}>t\vert u)\sim\bar A(u)\,\bar F(t)$, and
        \begin{equation*}
         \lim_{t\to\infty}\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}\bigl(\abs{v}>tx\,\big\vert\,\abs{v}>t,\,u\bigr)=x^{-\nu}
         \qquad\text{for every }x\ge1.
        \end{equation*}
        In particular $\E_{\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}}\abs{v}^{p}=\infty$ for every $p>\nu$.
  \item If the score is coercive at $u$, then
        $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\abs{v}>t\vert u)=o\bigl(t^{-\beta}\bar F(t)\bigr)$ for
        every $\beta\ge0$.
 \end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:tail}.
\end{proof}

Part (ii) is the central point. A saturating score passes exactly one number
per direction to the tail of the forecast, the constant $A(u,\theta)$. It can
change how \emph{likely} the forecast considers an extreme future in a given
direction, but not how \emph{large} that future is once it is extreme: beyond
the scale at which the score has levelled off, the forecast is a multiple of
the marginal, with the tail index $\nu$ of the marginal, for every past. No
choice of encoders, and no amount of training, lets such a forecast be
lighter-tailed than climatology. Whether this is a defect depends on the data,
and for heavy-tailed time series it typically is, because the past is
informative about the scale of the future.

\begin{lemma}[The Past Lightens the Tail]
 \label{lem:tprocess}
 Let $(u,v)$ be jointly elliptical Student-$t$ with $\nu$ degrees of freedom
 and a positive definite scale matrix. Then for every $u$ the conditional
 $\mu_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$ is an elliptical Student-$t$ distribution with
 $\nu+d_u$ degrees of freedom, and there is a continuous function
 $\ell^{\star}(u,\cdot):S\to\RR$ such that
 $\log r(u,t\theta')+d_u\log t\to\ell^{\star}(u,\theta)$ whenever $t\to\infty$
 and $\theta'\to\theta$. Thus the true conditional has tail index $\nu+d_u$,
 and the exact one-way score $\log r+c(u)$ has log-decay rate $\beta=d_u$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:tprocess}.
\end{proof}

Each observed coordinate of the past raises the tail index of the conditional
by one: an extreme future is far less likely after an ordinary past than it is
a priori, and $\log r$ records this by decreasing like $-d_u\log\abs{v}$. The
same computation applies to any Gaussian scale mixture whose mixing density is
regularly varying, the standard model of volatility clustering.

\begin{corollary}[No Standard Score Has the Right Tail]
 \label{cor:trichotomy}
 Let the data be as in \Cref{lem:tprocess} and the encoders as in
 \Cref{ex:profiles}, and write
 $\varrho_t(u)\define\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\abs{v}>t\vert u)/\mu_{v\vert u}(\abs{v}>t\vert u)$
 for the factor by which the model over-forecasts the event $\{\abs{v}>t\}$.
 \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item For the inner product $\sdot$ the model does not exist as soon as
        $\IP{f(u)}{g_\infty(\theta)}>0$ for some $\theta$ (\Cref{prop:relu}).
  \item For a saturating score, such as $\sncos$ or the inner product with a
        $\tanh$ future encoder, $\varrho_t(u)\to\infty$ at the rate $t^{d_u}$.
  \item For a coercive score, such as $\sltwo$, $\varrho_t(u)\to0$.
  \item For the Student-$t$ kernel score \eqref{eq:tkernel}, $\varrho_t(u)$
        converges to a finite positive limit if and only if $b=d_u$.
 \end{enumerate}
 In cases (ii) and (iii), and in case (iv) with $b\ne d_u$, the one-way loss
 is strictly positive. In particular none of $\sdot$, $\sncos$ and $\sltwo$
 is well-specified for the one-way loss on this data with these encoders.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:tprocess}.
\end{proof}

The three standard scores thus bracket the truth: the inner product has no
tail at all, the cosine keeps the tail of the marginal, and $L^2$ removes it
entirely. The two errors are not equally costly. A coercive score errs where
the true conditional has almost no mass, since
$\mu_{v\vert u}(\abs{v}>t\vert u)$ decays like $t^{-(\nu+d_u)}$. A saturating
score errs by placing mass of order $\bar A(u)\,t^{-\nu}$ where there should
be almost none.

\paragraph{A family with the right tail.}
In the Gaussian case, adding past-only and future-only quadratic terms to the
inner product made every loss well-specified (\Cref{tab:families}). The
heavy-tailed counterpart replaces quadratics by their logarithms.

\begin{proposition}[A Well-Specified Family on Heavy Tails]
 \label{prop:logquad}
 Let $(u,v)$ be jointly elliptical Student-$t$ with $\nu$ degrees of freedom
 and scale matrix $\Sigma$, and let $d=d_u+d_v$. Split the symmetric square
 root of $\Sigma^{-1}/\nu$ into its first $d_u$ and last $d_v$ columns,
 $(\Sigma^{-1}/\nu)^{1/2}=[\,G,\,-H\,]$, and let
 $G_0=(\nu\Sigma_{uu})^{-1/2}$, $H_0=(\nu\Sigma_{vv})^{-1/2}$. Then
 \begin{equation}
  \log r(u,v)=-\frac{\nu+d}{2}\log\bigl(1+\abs{Gu-Hv}^{2}\bigr)
  +\frac{\nu+d_u}{2}\log\bigl(1+\abs{G_0u}^{2}\bigr)
  +\frac{\nu+d_v}{2}\log\bigl(1+\abs{H_0v}^{2}\bigr)+\mathrm{const}.
  \label{eq:logquad}
 \end{equation}
 Consequently the family of \emph{log-quadratic scores}
 \begin{equation}
  s(u,v)=-\frac{b}{2}\log\bigl(1+\abs{f(u)-g(v)}^{2}\bigr)
  +\frac{b_u}{2}\log\bigl(1+\abs{f_0(u)}^{2}\bigr)
  +\frac{b_v}{2}\log\bigl(1+\abs{g_0(v)}^{2}\bigr),
  \qquad b,b_u,b_v\ge0,
  \label{eq:logquadfamily}
 \end{equation}
 with \emph{linear} encoders $f,g,f_0,g_0$, is well-specified for the one-way,
 two-way and joint losses under every divergence. With injective linear
 encoders $g,g_0$, its members with $b\ge b_v$ have log-decay rate $b-b_v$,
 so by \Cref{thm:tail} the forecast has tail index $\nu+b-b_v$; for the data
 this is $\nu+d_u$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:logquad}.
\end{proof}

The first term of \eqref{eq:logquadfamily} is the Student-$t$ kernel score
\eqref{eq:tkernel}; the other two play the role of the quadratic terms of
\Cref{tab:families}, to which the family reduces as $\nu\to\infty$, because
$\tfrac{\nu+d}{2}\log(1+x/\nu)\to x/2$. On heavy-tailed elliptical data it is
therefore the logarithm of a quadratic, not the quadratic itself, that a
score must be able to represent, and it is the difference $b-b_v$ of two
learned exponents that carries the tail index of the forecast.

\paragraph{Saturating low and saturating high.}
\Cref{cor:trichotomy} shows that a saturating score cannot have the right
tail. How wrong the tail is depends on the level at which the score
saturates, that is, on $\bar A(u)$. For flexible encoders the contrastive
loss itself chooses the lowest possible level.

\begin{proposition}[The KL-Optimal Bounded Score Is the Clipped Log Ratio]
 \label{prop:clip}
 Fix $u$ with $\KL{\mu_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)}{\mu_v}<\infty$, and let $s$ be
 any score whose tilting factor $\rho=e^{s(u,\cdot)}/Z(u)$ satisfies
 $m\le\rho\le M$ for constants $0<m\le1\le M$. Then there is $\lambda>0$ such
 that
 \begin{equation}
  \rho^{\star}\define\min\bigl\{\max\{r(u,\cdot)/\lambda,\;m\},\;M\bigr\}
  \label{eq:clip}
 \end{equation}
 integrates to one under $\mu_v$, and the model $\rho^{\star}\mu_v$ satisfies
 $\KL{\mu_{v\vert u}}{\rho^{\star}\mu_v}\le\KL{\mu_{v\vert u}}{\rho\,\mu_v}$,
 with equality only if $\rho=\rho^{\star}$ almost everywhere. In terms of
 scores: for every score whose oscillation $\sup_vs(u,v)-\inf_vs(u,v)$ is at
 most $2\kappa$ there is a score of the form
 $s^{\star}(u,v)=\min\{\max\{\log r(u,v)+c(u),\,a(u)\},\,a(u)+2\kappa\}$, the
 log density ratio clipped to a window of width $2\kappa$, whose one-way loss
 is no larger.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:clip}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[A Flexible Bounded Score Saturates at Its Floor]
 \label{cor:floor}
 In the setting of \Cref{prop:clip}, suppose that the past lightens the tail,
 $r(u,v)\to0$ as $\abs{v}\to\infty$. Then $\rho^{\star}=m=\inf_v\rho^{\star}$
 outside a bounded set. The clipped score is saturating with constant ray
 profile and tail amplification $A(u,\theta)\equiv m\le1$: of all scores with
 the same bounds, the KL-optimal one gives extreme futures the smallest
 weight that it gives to anything.
\end{corollary}

Boundedness alone is therefore not what makes a score over-weight extreme
futures. A flexible score that is bounded on both sides, trained to its
population optimum, saturates at its floor; the forecast still has the tail
index $\nu$ of the marginal (\Cref{cor:tailindex}), but with a constant $m$
that is at most $e^{-2\kappa}$ times the largest tilting factor and is
negligible when $\kappa$ is large, and the weight that a bank places on a tail
set converges to at most its marginal mass (\Cref{thm:exposure}(iii) below). This is the population-level reason why the normalized cosine
with flexible encoders performs well on heavy tails although its learned
scale $\kappa$, and with it the worst-case bound of \Cref{prop:twosided}, is
large (\Cref{ssec:banksweep}). A score saturates \emph{high}
when its architecture cannot place extreme futures at the floor. The next
remark shows that the inner product with a bounded future encoder is of this
kind.

\begin{remark}[Where Extreme Futures Land in Embedding Space]
 \label{rem:corners}
 For a two-tower score, the ray profile records where the future encoder
 sends extreme futures. Let $g$ be as in \Cref{ex:profiles} and let
 $\sigma(\theta)\define\operatorname{sign}g_\infty(\theta)\in\{\pm1\}^{d_z}$.
 \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \emph{Unbounded inner product.} $g(t\theta)$ leaves every bounded
        set, and the score is unbounded.
  \item \emph{$L^2$.} $g(t\theta)$ leaves every bounded set, hence moves
        away from every $f(u)$, and the score tends to $-\infty$.
  \item \emph{Normalized cosine.} $g(t\theta)/\abs{g(t\theta)}$ converges to
        a point of the unit sphere, on which all embeddings lie. Extreme
        futures have no special position: $\ell(u,\theta)$ equals the
        supremum $\kappa$ of the score only if $f(u)$ is exactly parallel to
        $g_\infty(\theta)$. If $g$ is linear the score is constant along
        rays, $A(u,\theta)=\rho(u,t\theta)$ for all $t>0$, and the tail is
        over-weighted exactly as much as the bulk in the same direction.
  \item \emph{Inner product with a $\tanh$ future encoder.}
        $\tanh g(t\theta)$ converges to the vertex $\sigma(\theta)$ of the
        cube $[-1,1]^{d_z}$ that contains all future embeddings. A vertex
        maximizes a linear functional over the cube, so the profile
        $\ell(u,\theta)=\kappa\IP{f(u)}{\sigma(\theta)}$ equals the supremum
        $\kappa\abs{f(u)}_{1}$ of the score for every past with
        $\operatorname{sign}f(u)=\sigma(\theta)$. For such a past
        $A(u,\theta)=\sup_v\rho(u,v)>1$: the most extreme futures in
        direction $\theta$ are, at every radius beyond the saturation scale,
        the most over-weighted futures in the whole space. Moreover
        $\ell(u,\theta)$ is linear in $f(u)$, so a direction that sits at the
        floor for one past sits at the ceiling for a past with the opposite
        embedding; with an odd past encoder, such as a linear one, the two
        pasts $u$ and $-u$ are of this kind, and the clipped optimum of
        \Cref{prop:clip}, which sits at the floor in every direction for
        every past, is out of reach.
 \end{enumerate}
 The contrastive loss makes case (iv) the typical one for the pasts that
 matter. A matched pair whose future is extreme, $v=t\theta$ beyond the
 saturation scale, has score $\kappa\IP{f(u)}{\sigma(\theta)}$, and
 increasing it aligns the signs of $f(u)$ with $\sigma(\theta)$. The pasts
 that precede extreme futures are thereby trained to point at the vertex
 that \emph{all} extreme futures in that direction share, whatever their
 size.
\end{remark}

With linear future encoders and elliptical data, the setting of
\cite{baptista2025mathematical} extended to heavy tails, these statements
hold at every radius and not only asymptotically.

\begin{proposition}[Linear Future Encoders Cannot Forecast Size]
 \label{prop:linearsat}
 Let $\mu_v$ be elliptical about the origin with scale matrix $\Sigma_{vv}$,
 so that the radius $R\define\abs{\Sigma_{vv}^{-1/2}v}$ is independent of the
 direction $\Sigma_{vv}^{-1/2}v/R$, and let $g$ be linear. Fix $u$ with
 $Z(u)<\infty$.
 \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item For the normalized cosine $\sncos$, with any past encoder,
        $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(R>t\vert u)=\mu_v(R>t)$ for every $t\ge0$: the
        forecast of the size of the future is climatology, for every past.
  \item For $s=\kappa\IP{f(u)}{\hat g(v)}$ with $\hat g=g$ (inner product)
        or $\hat g=\tanh g$, the score is odd in $v$, and for every
        symmetric set $B=-B$,
        \begin{equation*}
         \nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(B\vert u)=\frac{\E_{\mu_v}\bigl[\cosh s(u,v)\,\indicator_B(v)\bigr]}{\E_{\mu_v}\bigl[\cosh s(u,v)\bigr]}.
        \end{equation*}
        If, in addition, $\abs{s(u,rv)}$ is non-decreasing in $r>0$ for every
        $v$, as it is for the inner product, and for the $\tanh$ inner
        product when $d_z=1$, then
        $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(R>t\vert u)\ge\mu_v(R>t)$ for every $t\ge0$: the
        forecast of the size of the future is at least as heavy as
        climatology, for every past.
 \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:linearsat}.
\end{proof}

Part (i) is the future-side counterpart of the observation in
\Cref{ssec:lossvariants} that a linear normalized cosine loses the level of
the past: it has $\bar A(u)=1$ exactly, and its error does not depend on the
bank size. Part (ii) says that an odd score can lower the weight of extreme
futures on one side only by raising it on the other.

\paragraph{What a finite bank shows.}
\Cref{thm:tail} describes the limit $N\to\infty$ of the bank forecast. The
next result describes how that limit is approached, and explains why the
defect is invisible with a small bank.

\begin{theorem}[Exposure by the Bank]
 \label{thm:exposure}
 Fix $u$ and let the bank satisfy \Cref{dass:bank}. Let $T\subseteq\VV$ be
 measurable with $\pi\define\mu_v(T)\in(0,1)$, and write
 $P_N\define1-(1-\pi)^{N}$ for the probability that the bank contains a future
 in $T$.
 \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \emph{Ceiling.} For every score $s$, admissible or not,
        $\E\,\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)\le P_N\le N\pi$.
  \item \emph{Growth.} Let $Z(u)<\infty$, let
        $\rho\define\Dd\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}/\Dd\mu_v=e^{s(u,\cdot)}/Z(u)$, and
        suppose $\rho\ge a$ on $T$ for some $a\ge1$. Then
        \begin{equation}
         \E\,\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)\;\ge\;P_N\,\frac{a}{a+N-1}.
         \label{eq:growth}
        \end{equation}
  \item \emph{Saturation.} If in addition $\rho\le b$ on $T$ and
        $\chi^{2}\define\E_{\mu_v}[\rho^{2}]-1<\infty$, then for every $N\ge2$
        \begin{equation}
         \E\,\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)\;\le\;\bigl(4b+8\chi^{2}\bigr)\,\pi,
         \label{eq:saturation}
        \end{equation}
        and $\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)\to\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)\in[a\pi,b\pi]$
        almost surely.
 \end{enumerate}
 Consequently, whenever $N\pi\le1$, the \emph{over-forecast factor}
 $\mathrm{OF}_N(T)\define\E\,\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)/\pi$ satisfies
 \begin{equation}
  \tfrac14\min(N,a)\;\le\;\mathrm{OF}_N(T)\;\le\;\min\bigl(N,\;4b+8\chi^{2}\bigr).
  \label{eq:minlaw}
 \end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:exposure}.
\end{proof}

The weight that a bank forecast places on a set of extreme futures therefore
follows a $\min(N,A)$ law. As long as the bank is smaller than the tilting
factor on $T$, the weight grows \emph{linearly in the bank size}, at the
fastest rate that any score can achieve; once the bank exceeds the tilting
factor, the weight stops growing and settles at its population value. The
three kinds of score differ only in the height at which this happens. For an
inadmissible score there is no ceiling, the weight on $T$ grows until
$N\pi\approx1$, and the forecast collapses (\Cref{prop:collapse}). For a
saturating score the ceiling is the tail amplification: on a tail set
$T=\{\abs{v}>t,\ v/\abs{v}\in B\}$ the constants $a$ and $b$ may be taken
arbitrarily close to the infimum and supremum of $A(u,\cdot)$ over $B$ when
$t$ is large and the convergence in \ref{R2} is uniform on $B$. A bank of
size $N\ll A(u,\theta)$ cannot reveal the over-weighting in direction
$\theta$; a bank of size $N\gtrsim A(u,\theta)$ reveals all of it. For a
coercive score $\rho\to0$ on tail sets and the limit weight is $o(\pi)$.
Since the true conditional gives $T$ the mass $\bar r_T\,\pi$, with
$\bar r_T\define\mu_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)/\mu_v(T)$ the average density ratio on
$T$, the forecast probability of the extreme event exceeds the true one by a
factor of order $\min(N,A)/\bar r_T$, and $\bar r_T\to0$ for tail sets in the
setting of \Cref{lem:tprocess}.

\paragraph{The cost in energy score.}
The following elementary bound converts weight on a far set into energy
distance. Let $c_0\define2-\sqrt2$, and for a unit vector $e$ and $t>0$ let
$H_{t,e}\define\{x\in\RR^{d_v}:\abs{x}\ge t,\ \IP{x}{e}\ge0\}$ be the part of the
half-space in direction $e$ outside the ball of radius $t$.

\begin{lemma}[Energy Distance Charges Mass Times Distance]
 \label{lem:energytail}
 Let $p,q$ be probability measures on $\RR^{d_v}$ with finite first moments.
 Then for every unit vector $e$ and every $t>0$,
 \begin{equation}
  \DE(p,q)\;\ge\;\frac{1}{2t}\Bigl(c_0\,t\,q(H_{t,e})-2\,\E_{p}\abs{X}\Bigr)_{+}^{2}.
  \label{eq:energytail}
 \end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:energytail}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Energy Cost of a Plateau]
 \label{thm:energycost}
 In the setting of \Cref{thm:exposure}(ii), let $p\define\mu_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$
 have $m_p\define\E_p\abs{v}<\infty$, and let $T\subseteq H_{t,e}$ for some
 unit vector $e$ and some $t>0$. Let $y\sim p$ be independent of the bank and
 let
 \begin{equation*}
  \mathcal{E}_N(u)\define\E\bigl[\mathrm{ES}(\nu^{N}_{v\vert u},y)\bigr]-\E\bigl[\mathrm{ES}(p,y)\bigr]
  =\tfrac12\,\E\,\DE\bigl(p,\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}\bigr)
 \end{equation*}
 be the expected energy score of the bank forecast in excess of that of the
 true conditional. Then
 \begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}_N(u)\;\ge\;\frac{P_N}{4t}\Bigl(c_0\,t\,\frac{a}{a+N-1}-2m_p\Bigr)_{+}^{2}.
  \label{eq:energycost}
 \end{equation}
 In particular, if $c_0t\ge8m_p$, then
 $\mathcal{E}_N(u)\ge\frac{c_0^{2}}{128}\,N\pi\,t$ for every $N\le\min(a,1/\pi)$.
 For the limiting model,
 $\tfrac12\DE(p,\nu^{s}_{v\vert u})\ge\frac{1}{4t}\bigl(c_0t\,\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)-2m_p\bigr)_{+}^{2}$,
 where the left-hand side is $+\infty$ if $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}$ has no first
 moment.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:energycost}.
\end{proof}

Taken together, \Cref{thm:exposure,thm:energycost} are the precise form of
the statement that a larger bank exposes a score which saturates high. For
bank sizes up to the tail amplification, the expected excess energy score
contributed by a plateau grows at least linearly in $N$. Beyond it the bound
\eqref{eq:energycost} loses its force, and for a reason: once the bank holds
many plateau futures they share the excess weight, the forecast stops jumping
between banks, and the error decreases to the population error of
\Cref{thm:tail}, for which the last display of \Cref{thm:energycost} is a
lower bound. The contribution of a single plateau to the error curve is
therefore a bump centred at $N\approx A$, on top of the usual $O(1/N)$ decay
of the self-normalized estimator in the bulk. \Cref{tab:plateau} and
\Cref{fig:plateau} verify this for fixed scores. In a trained model the amplification $A(u,\theta)$ varies over
many orders of magnitude across pasts and directions
(\Cref{ssec:amplification}), the bumps overlap, and the error rises over the
whole range of bank sizes that can be afforded. The same lemma gives the cost
of collapse.

\begin{corollary}[Cost of Collapse]
 \label{cor:collapsecost}
 Let $s(u,v)=\IP{a(u)}{v}$ with $a(u)\ne0$, let $\mu_v$ be an elliptical
 Student-$t$ distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom, and fix
 $y\in\RR^{d_v}$. Then the energy score of the bank forecast grows like
 $N^{1/\nu}$: for every $\epsilon>0$ there is $c>0$ such that
 $\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathrm{ES}(\nu^{N}_{v\vert u},y)\ge c\,N^{1/\nu}\bigr)\ge1-\epsilon$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:energycost}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why training does not remove a plateau, and what would.}
The following proposition extends \Cref{prop:klblind} from a point mass to an
arbitrary tail set, and separates what the two divergences charge for.

\begin{proposition}[KL Charges for Mass, Energy for Mass Times Distance]
 \label{prop:klvsenergy}
 Fix $u$, let $p=\mu_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$ and $q=\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$
 with $\KL{p}{q}<\infty$, and let $T$ be measurable with
 $p(T)\le q(T)\eqqcolon\varepsilon<1$. For $h\in\RR$ let $q_h$ be the model of
 the score $s-h\indicator_T$, which lowers the score on $T$ and leaves it
 unchanged elsewhere.
 \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item The most that the Kullback--Leibler divergence can gain from
        correcting the mass on $T$ is
        \begin{equation*}
         \KL{p}{q}-\inf_{h\in\RR}\KL{p}{q_h}
         =p(T)\log\frac{p(T)}{q(T)}+(1-p(T))\log\frac{1-p(T)}{1-q(T)}
         \;\le\;-\log(1-\varepsilon),
        \end{equation*}
        whatever the location of $T$.
  \item If $T\subseteq H_{t,e}$ and $m_p=\E_p\abs{v}<\infty$, then
        $\DE(p,q)\ge\frac{1}{2t}\bigl(c_0t\,\varepsilon-2m_p\bigr)_{+}^{2}$,
        which grows linearly in $t$ for fixed $\varepsilon$.
 \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:klvsenergy}.
\end{proof}

A plateau that carries model mass $\varepsilon$ thus costs the KL-based losses
at most about $\varepsilon$ nats, however far away it is, while its cost in
energy distance grows with the distance once $\varepsilon t$ exceeds a few
multiples of the typical size $m_p$ of the future. Two further effects weaken
the signal that KL-based training receives. First, by
\Cref{thm:exposure}(i) with $N$ equal to the batch size, a batch contains a
future in $T$ with probability at most $N\pi$, whatever the score. Second, in
the contrastive loss the candidate set contains the matched future, whose own
tilting factor is large when the past is informative, so a plateau future in
the batch competes with it and takes little weight; in the bank forecast the
matched future is absent, and the same plateau future competes only with
unrelated ones. Training with a loss built from the bank forecast itself
removes both mismatches.

\begin{definition}[Bank Energy Loss]
 \label{def:bankenergy}
 For $K\ge2$ and an admissible score $s$, let
 $\nu^{K}_{v\vert u}$ be the bank forecast \eqref{eq:bank} built from
 $v_1,\ldots,v_K\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mu_v$, independent of the pair
 $(u,v)\sim\mu$. The \emph{bank energy loss} is
 \begin{equation}
  J^{\mathrm{E}}_{K}(s)\define\E\Bigl[\mathrm{ES}\bigl(\nu^{K}_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u),v\bigr)\Bigr],
  \qquad
  \mathrm{ES}(q,y)\define\E_{q}\abs{X-y}-\tfrac12\E_{q}\abs{X-X'}.
  \label{eq:bankenergy}
 \end{equation}
\end{definition}

For a weighted bank the energy score is a closed-form, differentiable function
of the scores,
$\mathrm{ES}(\nu^{K},y)=\sum_{j}w_j\abs{v_j-y}-\tfrac12\sum_{j,k}w_jw_k\abs{v_j-v_k}$,
so \eqref{eq:bankenergy} is trained from samples exactly as the contrastive
losses are: a batch of pairs supplies $(u,v)$, and $K$ further futures supply
the bank.

\begin{proposition}[The Bank Energy Loss Is the Energy-Distance Loss of a $K$-Bank]
 \label{prop:bankenergy}
 Suppose $\E_{\mu_v}\abs{v}<\infty$, and let
 $J^{\mathrm{E}}_{\star}\define\E_{(u,v)\sim\mu}\,\mathrm{ES}(\mu_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u),v)$
 be the energy score of the true conditional.
 \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $J^{\mathrm{E}}_{K}(s)-J^{\mathrm{E}}_{\star}
         =\tfrac12\,\E\,\DE\bigl(\mu_{v\vert u},\nu^{K}_{v\vert u}\bigr)\ge0$
        for every $K$ and every admissible score $s$.
  \item If $s$ is bounded, then
        $J^{\mathrm{E}}_{K}(s)\to J^{\mathrm{E}}_{\star}+\tfrac12\Jvu^{\DE}(s)$
        as $K\to\infty$, where $\Jvu^{\DE}$ is the one-way loss of
        \Cref{def:losses} with the energy distance.
  \item If, for $u$ in a set of positive $\mu_u$-measure, the score has a
        plateau as in \Cref{thm:energycost}, then
        $J^{\mathrm{E}}_{K}(s)-J^{\mathrm{E}}_{\star}$ is bounded below by the
        $\mu_u$-average of the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:energycost} with
        $N=K$.
 \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:bankenergy}.
\end{proof}

The bank energy loss is thus the energy-distance loss of
\cite[Rem.~3.5]{baptista2025mathematical} for the forecasting direction,
evaluated on the object that is used at test time. By part (iii) it charges a
plateau in proportion to its distance, which the contrastive losses do not.
It is not a replacement for them: a bank of $K$ futures cannot represent a
sharp conditional, so for small $K$ the minimizer of $J^{\mathrm{E}}_{K}$ is
flatter than $\log r$. In \Cref{ssec:energytraining} we therefore add it to
the contrastive loss, $J=J_{\mathrm{KL}}+\lambda J^{\mathrm{E}}_{K}$, so that
the KL term fits the bulk and the energy term disciplines the tails.

\subsection{Embedding Dimension and Correlation}
\label{ssec:dimension}

The results above concern the form of the score. We now ask how two further
quantities enter: the embedding dimension $d_z$, and the strength of the
dependence between past and future. On elliptical heavy-tailed data both
questions have exact answers, and they are linked, because the past informs
the future in two different ways: through \emph{correlation}, which moves the
centre of the forecast, and through a \emph{shared scale}, which sets its
size. The two are affected differently by the embedding dimension.

Throughout this subsection $(u,v)$ is jointly elliptical Student-$t$ with
$\nu$ degrees of freedom and a positive definite scale matrix $\Sigma$. Let
$\rho_1\ge\rho_2\ge\dots\ge0$ be the canonical correlations of past and
future, the singular values of $\Sigma_{uu}^{-1/2}\Sigma_{uv}\Sigma_{vv}^{-1/2}=U\,\mathrm{diag}(\rho)\,V^{\top}$,
let $k\define\operatorname{rank}\Sigma_{uv}$ be the number of them that are
non-zero, and let $a\define U^{\top}\Sigma_{uu}^{-1/2}u$ and
$b\define V^{\top}\Sigma_{vv}^{-1/2}v$ be the canonical coordinates.

\begin{proposition}[Correlation and Scale Contribute Separately]
 \label{prop:infosplit}
 The mutual information of past and future is
 \begin{equation}
  I(u;v)=\underbrace{-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\bigl(1-\rho_i^{2}\bigr)}_{\text{correlation}}
  \;+\;\underbrace{c_\nu(d_u,d_v)}_{\text{shared scale}},
  \label{eq:infosplit}
 \end{equation}
 where, with $\psi$ the digamma function and
 $e_\nu(j)\define\frac{\nu+j}{2}\bigl[\psi\bigl(\frac{\nu+j}{2}\bigr)-\psi\bigl(\frac{\nu}{2}\bigr)\bigr]$,
 \begin{equation}
  c_\nu(m,n)\define\log\frac{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{\nu}{2}\bigr)\,\Gamma\bigl(\frac{\nu+m+n}{2}\bigr)}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{\nu+m}{2}\bigr)\,\Gamma\bigl(\frac{\nu+n}{2}\bigr)}
  +e_\nu(m)+e_\nu(n)-e_\nu(m+n).
  \label{eq:cscale}
 \end{equation}
 The first term of \eqref{eq:infosplit} is the mutual information of a
 Gaussian with the same correlations. The second is the mutual information
 of an \emph{uncorrelated} Student-$t$ vector split into blocks of sizes $m$
 and $n$: it does not depend on $\Sigma$, it is positive, and it is strictly
 increasing in $m$ and in $n$. As $m,n\to\infty$,
 \begin{equation}
  c_\nu(m,n)=\frac12\log\frac{mn}{m+n}+C_\nu+o(1),\qquad
  C_\nu\define\frac\nu2+\log\Gamma\Bigl(\frac\nu2\Bigr)-\frac\nu2\,\psi\Bigl(\frac\nu2\Bigr)-\frac12\log(4\pi e).
  \label{eq:cscaleasym}
 \end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:dimension}.
\end{proof}

The identity \eqref{eq:infosplit} is known in the literature on elliptical
copulas \cite{calsaverini2009information,arellano2013shannon}; we use it to
separate what an embedding must carry. The scale term is not small. For
$\nu=3$ and windows of length $d_u=d_v=50$, $c_\nu(d_u,d_v)=1.20$ nats, while
it is small close to the Gaussian case ($0.06$ nats at $\nu=100$). The
expansion \eqref{eq:cscaleasym} explains its size: the common scale of past
and future is a single latent number, a window of length $m$ determines it
to a relative precision of order $m^{-1/2}$, and the information that two
windows share about it grows like half the logarithm of their length. The
tail results of \Cref{ssec:saturation} have the same origin and are equally
insensitive to correlation: by \Cref{lem:tprocess} the true conditional has
tail index $\nu+d_u$ whatever $\Sigma_{uv}$ is, including
$\Sigma_{uv}=0$.

\begin{example}[Correlation Between Subsequent Time Steps]
 \label{ex:ar}
 Let the underlying Gaussian series be stationary and Markov of order $p$,
 for instance an autoregression of order $p$, observed with or without
 independent noise, and let $u$ and $v$ be consecutive windows. The past
 enters the covariance with the future only through the $p$-dimensional
 state at the boundary, so $k=\operatorname{rank}\Sigma_{uv}\le p$, whatever
 the lengths of the windows. For an AR(1) series with lag-one correlation
 $\varphi$, observed without noise, $k=1$ and $\rho_1=\abs{\varphi}$, so that
 \begin{equation*}
  I(u;v)=-\tfrac12\log\bigl(1-\varphi^{2}\bigr)+c_\nu(d_u,d_v).
 \end{equation*}
 The correlation between subsequent time steps enters the first term only.
 With $\nu=3$ and $d_u=d_v=50$ the scale term is the larger of the two for
 every $\abs{\varphi}<0.95$: unless the series is very strongly correlated,
 most of what the past says about the future concerns its size, not its
 path. Lengthening the windows strengthens this, since the first term does
 not depend on their length and the second grows like its logarithm. At $\varphi=0$, a series of uncorrelated values with a common random
 scale, which is the elementary model of financial returns with volatility
 clustering, all of the information is of this kind.
\end{example}

\paragraph{What an embedding of dimension $d_z$ can carry.}
The next result holds for any data distribution. It expresses that a model
whose score sees the future only through an embedding can change the law of
the embedding and nothing else.

\begin{theorem}[Bottleneck]
 \label{thm:bottleneck}
 Let $\mu$ satisfy \Cref{ass:data}, let $h:\VV\to\RR^{d_z}$ be measurable,
 and let $s(u,v)=S(u,h(v))$ be an admissible score. Write $\mu_h$ for the
 law of $h(v)$ under $\mu_v$.
 \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item For every $u$ with $Z(u)<\infty$, the model is a mixture of the
        climatological conditionals of $v$ given its embedding,
        \begin{equation*}
         \nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\Dd v\vert u)=\int\mu_v\bigl(\Dd v\,\big\vert\,h(v)=w\bigr)\,\pi_u(\Dd w),
         \qquad\pi_u(\Dd w)\define\frac{e^{S(u,w)}}{Z(u)}\,\mu_h(\Dd w).
        \end{equation*}
        Under the model, the law of $v$ given $h(v)$ is the same for every
        past.
  \item $\Jvu^{\mathrm{KL}}(s)\ge I(u;v)-I(u;h(v))$, with equality if and only if
        $S(u,w)=\log r_h(u,w)+c(u)$, where $r_h$ is the density ratio
        \eqref{eq:ratio} of the pair $(u,h(v))$.
  \item If, in addition, the score sees the past only through an embedding,
        $s(u,v)=S(g(u),h(v))$ with $g:\UU\to\RR^{d_z}$ measurable, then
        $\Jvu^{\mathrm{KL}}(s)\ge I(u;v)-I(g(u);h(v))$.
 \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:dimension}.
\end{proof}

Parts (ii) and (iii) are data processing inequalities, and they apply
whatever the functional form of the score. On elliptical data the right-hand
sides can be evaluated.

\begin{corollary}[Linear Encoders Need Full Dimension, Nonlinear Encoders $k+1$]
 \label{cor:tdimension}
 Let the data be as in \Cref{prop:infosplit}.
 \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item Let $s(u,v)=S(u,Hv)$ with $H\in\RR^{d_z\times d_v}$, $d_z\le d_v$.
        Then
        \begin{equation}
         \Jvu^{\mathrm{KL}}(s)\;\ge\;-\frac12\sum_{i>d_z}\log\bigl(1-\rho_i^{2}\bigr)
         \;+\;c_\nu(d_u,d_v)-c_\nu(d_u,d_z),
         \label{eq:dimfloor}
        \end{equation}
        with equality when the rows of $H\Sigma_{vv}^{1/2}$ span the first
        $d_z$ canonical directions and $S=\log r_H+c(u)$. The first term
        vanishes as soon as $d_z\ge k$. The second is positive for every
        $d_z<d_v$. If the past encoder is linear as well, $s(u,v)=S(Gu,Hv)$
        with $G\in\RR^{d_z\times d_u}$ and $d_z\le\min(d_u,d_v)$, the bound
        holds with $c_\nu(d_z,d_z)$ in place of $c_\nu(d_u,d_z)$.
  \item For the embedding $h(v)=\bigl(b_1,\dots,b_k,\abs{b}^{2}\bigr)$ of
        dimension $k+1$, $I(u;h(v))=I(u;v)$: the bound of
        \Cref{thm:bottleneck}(ii) is zero, and $\log r(u,v)$ is a function
        of $u$ and $h(v)$. By symmetry, it depends on $u$ only through
        $\bigl(a_1,\dots,a_k,\abs{a}^{2}\bigr)$.
 \end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:dimension}.
\end{proof}

In the Gaussian limit only the first term of \eqref{eq:dimfloor} remains, and
an embedding of dimension $k$ suffices: for an AR(1) series a single
coordinate. With heavy tails the model must also know the size of the future,
$\abs{b}^{2}=v^{\top}\Sigma_{vv}^{-1}v$, which is one more number. A nonlinear
encoder can compute it, so heavy tails cost a nonlinear encoder one
dimension. A linear encoder cannot: it carries the size of the future only
if it is injective, so heavy tails cost a linear encoder $d_v-k$ dimensions,
and the cost of each missing dimension is the increment of $c_\nu(d_u,\cdot)$.
For $\nu=3$ and $d_u=50$, $c_\nu(d_u,d_z)$ equals $0.16$, $0.45$, $0.88$
and $1.20$ nats at $d_z=1,4,16,50$, so the loss decreases slowly and steadily
in $d_z$ instead of dropping to zero at $d_z=k$.

\paragraph{The tail of a model with a narrow linear embedding.}
The bottleneck also acts on the tail. By \Cref{thm:bottleneck}(i) the model
leaves the directions that the embedding does not see at their climatological
law given the ones it does see, and on heavy-tailed data that law is itself
heavy-tailed.

\begin{theorem}[A Linear Embedding Caps the Tail Index]
 \label{thm:tailcap}
 Let the data be as in \Cref{prop:infosplit}, let
 $H\in\RR^{d_z\times d_v}$ have rank $d_z<d_v$, and let $s(u,v)=S(u,Hv)$ for
 a measurable $S$. Let $\lambda>0$ be the smallest eigenvalue of
 $\Sigma_{vv}$ and let $T$ be a standard Student-$t$ vector in
 $\RR^{d_v-d_z}$ with $\nu+d_z$ degrees of freedom. Then for every $u$ with
 $Z(u)<\infty$ and every $t\ge0$,
 \begin{equation}
  \nu^{s}_{v\vert u}\bigl(\abs{v}>t\,\big\vert\,u\bigr)\;\ge\;
  \mathbb{P}\Bigl(\abs{T}>t\sqrt{\tfrac{\nu+d_z}{\lambda\nu}}\Bigr)\;\sim\;c\,t^{-(\nu+d_z)}
  \qquad(t\to\infty),
  \label{eq:tailcap}
 \end{equation}
 with $c>0$ independent of $u$ and of $S$. Consequently the forecast cannot
 have a tail index larger than $\nu+d_z$, and if $d_z<d_u$ the over-forecast
 factor of \Cref{cor:trichotomy} satisfies
 $\varrho_t(u)\ge c(u)\,t^{\,d_u-d_z}\to\infty$ for every past.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:dimension}.
\end{proof}

\Cref{thm:tailcap} does not follow from \Cref{thm:tail}: the mass in
\eqref{eq:tailcap} sits near the kernel of $H$, a set of directions of
angular measure zero on which the convergence \ref{R2} is not uniform. It
changes the classification of \Cref{cor:trichotomy}. With an injective
encoder the $L^2$ score is coercive and \emph{under}-forecasts extremes, and
the Student-$t$ kernel can match the true index $\nu+d_u$. With
$d_z<\min(d_u,d_v)$ both \emph{over}-forecast extremes, by a factor that
grows like a power of the threshold, whatever is learned. The true tail index
$\nu+d_u$ is out of reach of every score with a linear future encoder of
dimension below $\min(d_u,d_v)$. The past side obeys the same count at the
optimum: the best model that sees the past through a linear map $Gu$ of rank
$d_z<d_u$ is the conditional of $v$ given $Gu$, which by \Cref{lem:tprocess}
has tail index $\nu+d_z$. Each coordinate of the past that a linear encoder
discards costs one unit of tail index, whereas the nonlinear embedding of
\Cref{cor:tdimension}(ii), which keeps the radius $\abs{a}^{2}$, costs none.

\paragraph{When correlation is weak.}
The four standard scores with a linear future encoder share a structural
property: the past and the future interact only through a term that is odd
in the future. Such a term can move a forecast but cannot widen or narrow it.

\begin{proposition}[Odd Interactions Cannot Use the Scale]
 \label{prop:odd}
 Let $\mu$ satisfy \Cref{ass:data} and suppose that the true conditional is
 symmetric, $\mu_{v\vert u}(-B\vert u)=\mu_{v\vert u}(B\vert u)$ for every
 Borel set $B$ and $\mu_u$-almost every $u$. Let
 \begin{equation*}
  s(u,v)=\alpha(u)+\gamma(v)+o(u,v),\qquad\gamma(-v)=\gamma(v),\quad o(u,-v)=-o(u,v),
 \end{equation*}
 be admissible with $\gamma\in L^{1}(\mu_v)$ and $o\in L^{1}(\mu)$. Then
 \begin{equation*}
  \Jvu^{\mathrm{KL}}(s)\;\ge\;I(u;v)+\KL{\mu_v}{e^{\gamma}\mu_v/Z_\gamma}\;\ge\;I(u;v)=\Jvu^{\mathrm{KL}}(0),
  \qquad Z_\gamma\define\E_{\mu_v}e^{\gamma},
 \end{equation*}
 with equality throughout if and only if $o=0$ and $\gamma$ is constant
 almost everywhere. The best score of this form is the zero score, and its
 forecast is climatology for every past.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
 See \Cref{app:dimension}.
\end{proof}

With a linear future encoder without bias, the inner product, the inner
product with a $\tanh$ future encoder and the normalized cosine are odd in
the future ($\alpha=\gamma=0$), and the $L^2$ score is
$-\tfrac\kappa2\abs{f(u)}^{2}+\kappa\IP{f(u)}{g(v)}-\tfrac\kappa2\abs{g(v)}^{2}$,
of the form required. The past encoder and the embedding dimension are
arbitrary. The symmetry assumption holds for elliptical data with
$\Sigma_{uv}=0$. On the uncorrelated series of \Cref{ex:ar} these four
scores therefore learn nothing, although $c_\nu(d_u,d_v)$ nats are available,
and no increase of $d_z$ or of the capacity of the past encoder helps. The
Student-$t$ kernel and the log-quadratic scores are not of this form, and a
nonlinear future encoder breaks the oddness. For the normalized cosine a
bound of the same kind holds at every correlation: by
\Cref{prop:linearsat}(i) its forecast of the radius
$R=\abs{\Sigma_{vv}^{-1/2}v}$ is climatology, so
$\Jvu^{\mathrm{KL}}(\sncos)\ge I(u;R)$ by the data processing inequality.

\paragraph{Predictions.}
Taken together, the results of this subsection make three predictions for a
heavy-tailed series with $k$ non-zero canonical correlations, which we test
in \Cref{ssec:dimsweep}.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(D\arabic*),ref=D\arabic*]
 \item \label{D1} \emph{Scores with an odd interaction and a linear future
       encoder} gain nothing from an embedding dimension beyond $k$, and
       approach climatology as the correlation decreases.
 \item \label{D2} \emph{Scores that can represent the scale, with a linear
       future encoder,} improve steadily up to $d_z=d_v$, following
       \eqref{eq:dimfloor}, and over-forecast extremes when $d_z$ is small.
 \item \label{D3} \emph{With a nonlinear future encoder} a small embedding
       dimension, of order $k+1$, suffices.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{ssec:setup}

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
Every contrastive model is evaluated only through its own score, by building
the bank forecast \eqref{eq:bank} from a bank of real futures not used in
training. Forecasts are scored with the energy score \cite{gneiting2007strictly},
a proper scoring rule for multivariate probabilistic forecasts that rewards
both accuracy and calibrated uncertainty. On synthetic data each domain has
an exact optimal forecaster (oracle), and we report the energy score as a
percentage above that of the oracle ($0\%$ is perfect). On real data we report
\emph{skill}, the fractional reduction in energy score against a reference
forecast (higher is better).

\paragraph{Synthetic domains.}
Past and future are windows of $50$ points of a smooth Gaussian process
observed with noise. Each synthetic domain has a parameter whose zero point
is this Gaussian process, the setting of \cite{baptista2025mathematical}:
\begin{itemize}
 \item \emph{heavy tails}: an elliptical Student-$t$ process with $\nu$
       degrees of freedom ($\nu=\infty$ is the Gaussian process; $\nu=3$ is
       the heaviest used);
 \item \emph{bent GP}: a monotone warp of the Gaussian process, giving
       skewed futures and a nonlinear relation between past and future;
 \item \emph{double-well}: a stochastic process that jumps between two
       levels, so that the future can have two separate likely outcomes.
\end{itemize}
Each synthetic setting uses $200$k training pairs, a bank of $50$k held-out
futures, $500$ test cases and $2$ seeds, so that differences below about
$1\%$ are noise. The tail experiments of
\Cref{ssec:banksweep,ssec:amplification,ssec:logtail,ssec:energytraining}
use banks of up to $200$k futures and more seeds, as stated there.

\paragraph{Real data.}
We use one year (2020) of 10-minute readings from the Jena weather station. We
forecast the next 6\,h of temperature from the last day of six variable
groups, and the next 6\,h of rain from past pressure, wind and moisture.
Three larger heavy-tailed series are introduced in \Cref{ssec:realheavy}.

\paragraph{Encoders.}
Linear encoders reproduce the setting of \cite{baptista2025mathematical}.
Flexible encoders are two-layer MLPs of width $256$ with GELU activations and
a linear shortcut.

\subsection{Loss Variants}
\label{ssec:lossvariants}

\paragraph{Linear encoders: the loss and the score both matter.}
On the Gaussian process, the inner-product score is $+5.6\%$ above perfect
whichever direction it is trained in (\ref{P3}), and $+9.8\%$ when trained
jointly (\ref{P4}). Its $90\%$ bands contain the truth $98\%$ of the time on
the hardest third of cases, i.e.\ they are too wide (\ref{P1}). The $L^2$
score is at $+0.0\%$ in all three variants, consistent with the last row of
\Cref{tab:infoloss}. The linear normalized cosine is poor everywhere
($+12$--$23\%$), because it sees the past only through its direction and
loses its level.

\paragraph{Flexible encoders: the loss variant mostly stops mattering.}
With MLP encoders the range between the variants collapses
(\Cref{tab:variants}), as \Cref{thm:approxagreement} predicts, and \ref{P1}
fades: the coverage of the inner product on the hardest cases drops from
$98\%$ to $89\%$. The best models (joint $L^2$, normalized cosine) are within
about $1\%$ of perfect on every domain. The differences that remain are
small and consistently favour the joint loss; we return to them in
\Cref{ssec:finite}.

\subsection{Unbounded Scores on Heavy Tails}
\label{ssec:heavytails}

Every catastrophic failure we observed is an unbounded score on heavy tails
(\Cref{tab:heavy}). The linear inner product places essentially all its

unnormalized inner product scored $1.4$--$2.4$ times worse than climatology,
while $L^2$ and normalized cosine retained the shape ``mostly dry, sometimes a

\subsection{Bank-Size Sweep}
\label{ssec:banksweep}

The experiments in this and the next three subsections test the tail results
of \Cref{sec:tails} on the heavy-tailed domain ($\nu=3$). Each model is
trained as in \Cref{ssec:setup} ($15$k steps, batch size $256$) and then
frozen; only the bank changes. The forecast is rebuilt from held-out banks of
size $N=100$ up to $N=200{,}000$, with up to $4$ disjoint banks per size, on
the same $500$ test pasts. Every seed has its own training set, test pasts
and banks, and the tables report the mean over the seeds available (column
$n$). Besides the scores of \Cref{ex:scores} and the $\tanh$ inner product,
we train the two scores with a logarithmic tail suggested by
\Cref{ssec:saturation}: the Student-$t$ kernel score \eqref{eq:tkernel} with a
learnable exponent $b$, and the log-quadratic family
\eqref{eq:logquadfamily} with learnable $b,b_u,b_v$ and linear maps $f_0,g_0$.

\paragraph{Does a larger bank help or hurt?}
\Cref{thm:bank} and \Cref{prop:collapse} predict that the unbounded inner
product turns around and gets worse as the bank grows;
\Cref{thm:exposure,thm:energycost} predict the same, more mildly, for a score
that saturates high, and no turnaround for coercive and logarithmic scores.
\Cref{tab:banksweep} and the top row of \Cref{fig:sweep} confirm all three.
The unbounded inner product is best at a small bank and then deteriorates
steadily, by orders of magnitude with linear encoders, as
\Cref{cor:collapsecost} predicts. The inner product with a $\tanh$ future
encoder exists as a model and is far better, but it also turns around. The
normalized cosine with MLP encoders, joint $L^2$, and both logarithmic scores
improve monotonically and level off. On the Gaussian control the unbounded
and the $\tanh$ inner products also improve and level off
(\Cref{tab:gaussiancontrol}), so the turnaround is caused by the tails and
not by the score alone.

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$\min(N,A)$}{min(N,A)} Law in Trained Models}
\label{ssec:amplification}

it can be tested directly. For each frozen model we record two quantities.
The first is the \emph{over-forecast factor} of the event
$T_p=\{\abs{v}>t_p\}$, where $t_p$ is the $(1-p)$ quantile of $\abs{v}$ under
the marginal: the bank weight on $T_p$, averaged over the test pasts, divided
by $p$. The second is a \emph{ray probe} of the tail amplification, which
needs no tail data: we evaluate the frozen score on $1000$ bank futures
multiplied by $c=1000$, subtract the estimate of $\log Z(u)$ from the largest
bank, and average over the directions to obtain $\bar A(u)$ of
\eqref{eq:amplification}.

\subsection{Scores with a Logarithmic Tail}
\label{ssec:logtail}

\Cref{cor:trichotomy} and \Cref{prop:logquad} say that on this data the
score should decay logarithmically in the future, and that the log-quadratic
family contains the truth with \emph{linear} encoders. Both predictions hold
(\Cref{tab:banksweep}). With linear encoders, the log-quadratic family is
the only one that comes close to the oracle; linear $L^2$, the well-specified
family of the Gaussian case, levels off several points above it. With MLP
encoders, the Student-$t$ kernel score trained with the \emph{one-way} loss
matches the best models, whereas one-way $L^2$ does not; the one-way loss is
the well-specified variant for forecasting, and with a score that has the
right tail it no longer needs the regularizing effect of the joint loss
discussed in \Cref{ssec:finite}. The learned exponents are finite and of the
order of the dimension of the past
%%BEGIN:exponents
($b\approx28$ for the one-way Student-$t$ kernel with MLP encoders, against $d_u=50$),
%%END:exponents
so the models choose a polynomial, not a Gaussian, tail.

\subsection{Training with the Bank Energy Loss}
\label{ssec:energytraining}

Finally we train with the loss $J_{\mathrm{KL}}+\lambda J^{\mathrm{E}}_{K}$ of
\Cref{ssec:saturation}: the contrastive loss of each score, plus the energy
score of the bank forecast built from $K=512$ training futures drawn afresh
at every step, in units of the climatological spread
$\E\abs{v-v'}$, with $\lambda=10$. Nothing else changes. The lower rows of
each block of \Cref{tab:banksweep,tab:overforecast} give the result.

For the $\tanh$ inner product with MLP encoders the turnaround disappears:
the model now improves monotonically with the bank and ends within about a
point of the oracle, next to the best models, and its over-forecast factor
drops below one. The loss has taught the encoders to place extreme futures
low. With linear encoders the improvement is partial, as it must be: by
\Cref{prop:linearsat}(ii) and \Cref{rem:corners} no parameter of that family
places them low for every past. The same holds for the unbounded inner
product, for which the energy term reduces the damage but cannot remove it:
a bank of $K=512$ futures exposes a plateau or a growth direction only up to
amplification of order $K$ (\Cref{thm:energycost}), and the family has no
admissible informative member (\Cref{prop:relu}). Training with the bank
energy loss alone, without the contrastive term, also removes the turnaround
but gives a flatter forecast that levels off slightly higher, as anticipated
after \Cref{prop:bankenergy}. For scores that already have a harmless tail
the added term changes little.

The population analysis bounds what joint and two-way $L^2$ can lose to
rigidity: in the scalar Gaussian, at most about $3/8$ (joint) or $1/3$
(two-way) of $I(u;v)$ (\Cref{prop:scalar}). On rain, $I(u;v)$ is tiny (the
best model beats climatology by $1\%$), so that population bias is negligible,
and the observed gaps stem from finite-sample estimation. One-way $L^2$ has no
structural constraint preventing $S$ from becoming overly large, and on rain its
forecasts collapsed (minimum $\ESS$ of $1.6$ with linear encoders, median
$\ESS$ of $3$ with MLPs). Two-way and joint $L^2$ impose such a check: by the
Schur complement condition $S\succeq A^{\top}R^{+}A$, a sharp forward
conditional requires a correspondingly sharp backward conditional, which the
two-way loss penalizes, while the joint loss additionally penalizes distortion
of the model marginals. Thus, the structural constraint of the joint $L^2$
objective acts as an implicit regularizer against over-sharpening in the
low-signal, finite-sample regime. Characterizing this bias--variance trade-off
analytically is an interesting direction for future work.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

We have viewed a contrastive forecaster as a learned tilting of the marginal
distribution of futures, and asked which of its design choices survive in the
population limit. The loss and the divergence do not: every variant aims at
the log density ratio, and for flexible encoders on compact domains a single
score drives every KL-based variant to zero. The score does survive, and
precisely through its tails. A score bounded above defines a valid model for
every data distribution, and its bank forecast converges; a score that grows
in the future defines no model on heavy-tailed data, even with ReLU or GELU
encoders, and its bank forecast collapses onto the most extreme future. This
identifies the tail bound in the non-compact universal approximation result of
\cite{stuart2026expressivity} as necessary rather than technical.

Between these two cases lie the scores that are bounded but level off along
extreme futures, and for them boundedness is not enough. The tail of the
forecast is the tail of the marginal times the tail amplification of the
score, so the forecast has the tail index of climatology for every past,
while heavy-tailed data with a random scale have a conditional that is
lighter by one unit of tail index per observed coordinate. A bank of size
$N$ reveals an amplification $A$ only once $N\gtrsim A$: the weight on
extreme futures follows a $\min(N,A)$ law, and the excess energy score grows
linearly in $N$ up to that point. The population optimum of the contrastive
loss over bounded scores is the clipped log density ratio, which saturates
at its floor, so a score that saturates high does so because of its
architecture: an inner product with a bounded future encoder sends extreme
futures to the vertices of its range. The scores with the right tail decay
logarithmically, and a log-quadratic family is well-specified on elliptical
heavy-tailed data with linear encoders. Experiments on synthetic and real
series confirm the $\min(N,A)$ law, the benefit of a logarithmic tail, and
the effect of adding the energy score of the bank forecast to the training
loss.

\paragraph{Scope and limitations.}
\Cref{sec:invariance,sec:tails} are population-level statements about the
score, complemented by finite-bank results
(\Cref{thm:bank,prop:collapse,thm:exposure,thm:energycost}); they do not
describe the optimization that produces the score. In particular, that a
trained $\tanh$ inner product saturates high for the pasts that matter is
explained (\Cref{rem:corners}) and measured (\Cref{ssec:amplification}) but
not proved. \Cref{thm:tail} assumes regularly varying futures and scores with
a limit along rays; this covers elliptical heavy tails and encoders that are
positively homogeneous at infinity, but not, for instance, futures with a
point mass at zero such as rainfall, for which only the finite-bank results
apply. The constants in \Cref{thm:energycost} are not sharp. In
\Cref{tab:infoloss}, the population misspecification gap is quantified via
the KL divergence, whereas the empirical evaluations use the energy score,
which is less sensitive to variance miscalibration; the two metrics agree in
ordering though not in magnitude.

\paragraph{Open questions.}
First, the bank energy loss is the energy-distance loss of
\cite[Rem.~3.5]{baptista2025mathematical} restricted to the forecasting
direction and to a bank of fixed size $K$; its minimizer for finite $K$, and
the two-way and joint analogues, remain to be characterized. Second, it
remains to establish non-compact universal approximation theorems for
architectures whose scores have a prescribed tail, such as two-tower $L^2$,
Student-$t$ kernel or log-quadratic scores, without relying on truncation to
a compact set. Third, \Cref{lem:tprocess} suggests that the logarithmic decay
rate of the exact score equals the number of effective degrees of freedom of
the past that inform the scale of the future; making this precise beyond
scale mixtures, for instance for stochastic volatility models, would turn the
tail index of the forecast into a design parameter. Finally, a formal
finite-sample analysis of the regularization effect observed for the joint
$L^2$ loss in \Cref{ssec:finite} remains a worthwhile direction.

\subsection{Proof of \texorpdfstring{\Cref{prop:target}}{Proposition 3.1}}
\label{app:target}
Because $D(p,q)\ge0$ with equality only if $p=q$, each loss vanishes exactly
when the distributions that it compares are equal. The remainder of the
argument is therefore about equality of distributions and does not involve
$D$.

(i) $\Jvu^{D}(s)=0$ if and only if $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)=\mu_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$
for $\mu_u$-almost every $u$. Comparing densities with respect to $\mu_v$,
this reads $e^{s(u,\cdot)}/Z(u)=r(u,\cdot)$, that is,
$s=\log r+\log Z(u)$. Conversely, $s=\log r+c(u)$ gives
$Z(u)=e^{c(u)}\int r(u,v)\,\mu_v(\Dd v)=e^{c(u)}$, and hence the true
conditional. (ii) is symmetric. (iii) Since $\lambda_u,\lambda_v>0$,
$\Jtwo^{D}=0$ if and only if (i) and (ii) both hold, so that $c(u)=c'(v)$ for
$\muP$-almost every $(u,v)$. A function of $u$ alone that equals a function
of $v$ alone almost everywhere under a product measure is almost everywhere
constant. For the joint loss, $D(\mu,\nu^{s})=0$ if and only if
$\nu^{s}=\mu$, if and only if $e^{s}/Z=r$, that is, $s=\log r+\log Z$.
\qed

\subsection{Proof of \texorpdfstring{\Cref{prop:scalar}}{Proposition 3.7}}
\label{app:scalar}

$Q=\begin{pmatrix}g^{2}&-gh\\-gh&h^{2}\end{pmatrix}$ and, up to constants,

the cross term is minimized at $g=h$, leaving $t(1-\rho)-\log(1+t)$, which is
minimized at $t=\rho/(1-\rho)$. The forecast then has precision
$1+t/2=(2-\rho)/(2(1-\rho))$ and mean coefficient $\rho/(2-\rho)$. Expanding

again sets $g^{2}=h^{2}=\tau$, so that $\Jtwo=\Jvu$. The forecast has precision
$1+\tau$ and mean coefficient $\tau/(1+\tau)$, and up to constants
$2\Jvu=\tau(1-2\rho)+\frac{\tau^{2}}{1+\tau}-\log(1+\tau)$. Setting the
derivative to zero gives $x^{2}+x=2(1-\rho)$ for $x=1/(1+\tau)$, so
\[
 \tau=\frac{\sqrt{9-8\rho}-3+4\rho}{4(1-\rho)}=\tfrac23\rho+O(\rho^{2}).
\]
Expanding for small $\rho$ gives
$2\Jvu=\tfrac12\tau^{2}+(\rho-\tau)^{2}+O(\rho^{3})$, so

$\rho=0.5$ this gives $\tau=(\sqrt5-1)/2$ and $\Jvu/I(u;v)=0.1479$, matching
\Cref{tab:infoloss}.

\subsection{Proof of \texorpdfstring{\Cref{thm:bank}}{Theorem 4.5}}
\label{app:bank}
For bounded continuous $\varphi$, the strong law of large numbers gives
$\frac1N\sum_{j}e^{s(u,v_j)}\varphi(v_j)\to\int e^{s}\varphi\,\Dd\mu_v$ and
$\frac1N\sum_{j}e^{s(u,v_j)}\to Z(u)$ almost surely, since both integrands
are integrable when $Z(u)<\infty$. The ratio converges to
$\int\varphi\,\Dd\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}$, and a countable convergence-determining
class gives weak convergence. With $\rho=e^{s}/Z(u)$,
$N\sum_{j}w_j^{2}=\frac{\frac1N\sum_j\rho(v_j)^{2}}{(\frac1N\sum_j\rho(v_j))^{2}}\to\E_{\mu_v}[\rho^{2}]$.
If $Z(u)=\infty$, the strong law for nonnegative random variables gives
$\frac1N\sum_{j}e^{s(u,v_j)}\to\infty$.
\qed

\subsection{Proof of \texorpdfstring{\Cref{prop:collapse}}{Proposition 4.6}}
\label{app:collapse}
Let $X_{(1)}\ge X_{(2)}$ be the two largest scores and $b_N$ the
$1-1/N$ quantile of $X$, so that $b_N=N^{1/\alpha}\ell(N)$ with $\ell$ slowly
varying. By the extreme-value limit for regularly varying tails,
$(X_{(1)},X_{(2)})/b_N$ converges in distribution to

$\varepsilon>0$ there is $c>0$ with
$\mathbb{P}(X_{(1)}-X_{(2)}\ge cb_N)\ge1-\varepsilon$ for large $N$, and on
that event
$1-\max_j w_j\le\sum_{j\neq(1)}e^{X_j-X_{(1)}}\le Ne^{-cb_N}\to0$, because
$b_N$ grows polynomially.
\qed

\subsection{Proofs of \texorpdfstring{\Cref{thm:tail}}{Theorem 4.12} and \texorpdfstring{\Cref{cor:tailindex}}{Corollary 4.13}}
\label{app:tail}
\emph{Proof of \Cref{thm:tail}.}
Let $\mathbb{P}_t$ be the law of $(\abs{v}/t,\,v/\abs{v})$ under
$\mu_v(\,\cdot\mid\abs{v}>t)$, a probability measure on $[1,\infty)\times S$.
By \Cref{ass:rv}, $\mathbb{P}_t$ converges weakly to
$\mathbb{P}\define\mathrm{Par}(\nu)\otimes\Lambda$, where $\mathrm{Par}(\nu)$
has survival function $x^{-\nu}$ on $[1,\infty)$. By definition of the tilted
model,
\begin{equation*}
 \frac{\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}\bigl(\abs{v}>t,\,v/\abs{v}\in B\,\big\vert\,u\bigr)}{t^{-\beta}\bar F(t)}
 =\frac{1}{Z(u)}\int h_t(r,\theta)\,\indicator_B(\theta)\,\mathbb{P}_t(\Dd r,\Dd\theta),
 \qquad h_t(r,\theta)\define t^{\beta}e^{s(u,tr\theta)}.
\end{equation*}
Write $h_t(r,\theta)=r^{-\beta}\exp\bigl(s(u,tr\theta)+\beta\log(tr)\bigr)$.
By \ref{R1} there is $C<\infty$ with
$s(u,v)+\beta\log\abs{v}\le s(u,v)+\beta\log(1+\abs{v})\le C$, so
$0\le h_t\le e^{C}$ on $[1,\infty)\times S$ for every $t$. Let $S_0\subseteq S$
be the set of full $\Lambda$-measure on which \ref{R2} holds, and let
$(r_t,\theta_t)\to(r,\theta)$ with $r\ge1$ and $\theta\in S_0\setminus\partial B$.
Then $tr_t\to\infty$ and $\theta_t\to\theta$, so \ref{R2} gives
$s(u,tr_t\theta_t)+\beta\log(tr_t)\to\ell(u,\theta)$, hence
$h_t(r_t,\theta_t)\indicator_B(\theta_t)\to r^{-\beta}e^{\ell(u,\theta)}\indicator_B(\theta)\eqqcolon h(r,\theta)$.
The set $[1,\infty)\times(S_0\setminus\partial B)$ has $\mathbb{P}$-measure
one. The extended continuous mapping theorem \cite[Thm.~5.5]{billingsley1968convergence}
therefore gives $h_t\indicator_B$ under $\mathbb{P}_t$ converging in
distribution to $h$ under $\mathbb{P}$, and since the functions are uniformly
bounded, the expectations converge:
\begin{equation*}
 \int h_t\indicator_B\,\Dd\mathbb{P}_t\;\longrightarrow\;
 \int_{1}^{\infty}r^{-\beta}\,\nu r^{-\nu-1}\,\Dd r\int_{B}e^{\ell(u,\theta)}\,\Lambda(\Dd\theta)
 =\frac{\nu}{\nu+\beta}\int_{B}e^{\ell(u,\theta)}\,\Lambda(\Dd\theta).
\end{equation*}
Dividing by $Z(u)$ gives \eqref{eq:tail}.
\qed

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Proof of \Cref{cor:tailindex}.}
(i) Write $G(t,B)\define\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\abs{v}>t,\,v/\abs{v}\in B\vert u)$.
For $x\ge1$, apply \eqref{eq:tail} at $tx$ and at $t$ and use
$\bar F(tx)/\bar F(t)\to x^{-\nu}$, which is \eqref{eq:rv} with $B=S$:
\begin{equation*}
 \frac{G(tx,B)}{G(t,S)}
 =\frac{G(tx,B)}{(tx)^{-\beta}\bar F(tx)}\cdot\frac{t^{-\beta}\bar F(t)}{G(t,S)}\cdot x^{-\beta}\,\frac{\bar F(tx)}{\bar F(t)}
 \;\longrightarrow\;x^{-(\nu+\beta)}\,\frac{\int_BA(u,\theta)\,\Lambda(\Dd\theta)}{\bar A(u)},
\end{equation*}
which is \eqref{eq:rv} for the measure $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}$ with index
$\nu+\beta$ and angular measure $A\Lambda/\bar A$. The sets $B$ with
$\Lambda(\partial B)=0$ are continuity sets of this angular measure as well,
because it is absolutely continuous with respect to $\Lambda$. (ii) Take
$\beta=0$ and $B=S$ in \eqref{eq:tail} for the first claim and in (i) for the
second. If $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\abs{v}>t)\sim\bar A\,\bar F(t)$ with $\bar F$
regularly varying of index $-\nu$, then
$\E\abs{v}^{p}=\int_0^\infty pt^{p-1}\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\abs{v}>t)\,\Dd t=\infty$
for $p>\nu$. (iii) is \eqref{eq:tail} with $B=S$ and $A\equiv0$.
\qed

\subsection{Proofs of \texorpdfstring{\Cref{lem:tprocess}}{Lemma 4.14} and \texorpdfstring{\Cref{cor:trichotomy}}{Corollary 4.15}}
\label{app:tprocess}
\emph{Proof of \Cref{lem:tprocess}.}
Let $\Sigma$ be the scale matrix, with blocks $\Sigma_{uu},\Sigma_{uv},\Sigma_{vv}$,
and let $K\define\Sigma_{uv}^{\top}\Sigma_{uu}^{-1}$, $\Sigma_{v\vert u}\define\Sigma_{vv}-\Sigma_{uv}^{\top}\Sigma_{uu}^{-1}\Sigma_{uv}$
and $q_u\define u^{\top}\Sigma_{uu}^{-1}u$. The conditional of a multivariate
$t$ distribution is again multivariate $t$ \cite{kotz2004multivariate}:
$v\vert u$ has $\nu+d_u$ degrees of freedom, location $Ku$ (in the row convention $u^{\top}\Sigma_{uu}^{-1}\Sigma_{uv}$) and scale matrix
$\frac{\nu+q_u}{\nu+d_u}\Sigma_{v\vert u}$. Writing out the two densities,
\begin{equation*}
 p(v\vert u)=C_1(u)\Bigl[1+\frac{(v-Ku)^{\top}\Sigma_{v\vert u}^{-1}(v-Ku)}{\nu+q_u}\Bigr]^{-\frac{\nu+d_u+d_v}{2}},
 \qquad
 p(v)=C_2\Bigl[1+\frac{v^{\top}\Sigma_{vv}^{-1}v}{\nu}\Bigr]^{-\frac{\nu+d_v}{2}}.
\end{equation*}
For $v=t\theta'$ with $\theta'\to\theta\in S$ and $t\to\infty$, the first
bracket equals $t^{2}\bigl(\theta^{\top}\Sigma_{v\vert u}^{-1}\theta/(\nu+q_u)+o(1)\bigr)$
and the second $t^{2}\bigl(\theta^{\top}\Sigma_{vv}^{-1}\theta/\nu+o(1)\bigr)$.
Taking logarithms of the ratio,
\begin{equation*}
 \log r(u,t\theta')=-d_u\log t+\log\frac{C_1(u)}{C_2}
 -\frac{\nu+d_u+d_v}{2}\log\frac{\theta^{\top}\Sigma_{v\vert u}^{-1}\theta}{\nu+q_u}
 +\frac{\nu+d_v}{2}\log\frac{\theta^{\top}\Sigma_{vv}^{-1}\theta}{\nu}+o(1),
\end{equation*}
which defines the continuous function $\ell^{\star}(u,\cdot)$. A multivariate
$t$ distribution with $n$ degrees of freedom is regularly varying with index
$n$, so the conditional has index $\nu+d_u$.
\qed

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Proof of \Cref{cor:trichotomy}.}
(i) is \Cref{prop:relu}, which applies when $\IP{f(u)}{g_\infty(\theta)}>0$
for some $\theta$. By \Cref{lem:tprocess},
$\mu_{v\vert u}(\abs{v}>t\vert u)\sim c(u)\,t^{-(\nu+d_u)}$ with $c(u)>0$, while
$\bar F(t)\sim c\,t^{-\nu}$ with $c>0$. (ii) For a saturating score,
\Cref{cor:tailindex}(ii) gives
$\varrho_t(u)\sim\bar A(u)\,c\,t^{d_u}/c(u)\to\infty$; the scores of
\Cref{ex:profiles}(a),(b) have a finite ray profile, hence $\bar A(u)>0$.
(iii) For a coercive score, \Cref{cor:tailindex}(iii) with $\beta=d_u$ gives
$\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\abs{v}>t\vert u)=o(t^{-(\nu+d_u)})$. (iv) The score
\eqref{eq:tkernel} has log-decay rate $b$ and a finite ray profile, so by
\eqref{eq:tail} $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\abs{v}>t\vert u)\sim c'(u)\,t^{-(\nu+b)}$
with $c'(u)>0$, and $\varrho_t(u)\sim(c'(u)/c(u))\,t^{d_u-b}$. In cases
(ii)--(iv) with $b\ne d_u$ the model and the true conditional have different
tail indices, so they differ for every $u$ and the one-way loss is positive
by \Cref{prop:target}(i).
\qed

\subsection{Proofs of \texorpdfstring{\Cref{prop:logquad}}{Proposition 4.16}, \texorpdfstring{\Cref{prop:clip}}{Proposition 4.17} and \texorpdfstring{\Cref{cor:floor}}{Corollary 4.18}}
\label{app:logquad}
\emph{Proof of \Cref{prop:logquad}.}
With $z=(u,v)$, the joint density is proportional to
$(1+z^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}z/\nu)^{-(\nu+d)/2}$ and the marginal densities to
$(1+u^{\top}\Sigma_{uu}^{-1}u/\nu)^{-(\nu+d_u)/2}$ and
$(1+v^{\top}\Sigma_{vv}^{-1}v/\nu)^{-(\nu+d_v)/2}$, since the marginals of a
multivariate $t$ distribution are multivariate $t$ with the same degrees of
freedom \cite{kotz2004multivariate}. By the definitions of $G,H,G_0,H_0$,
$z^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}z/\nu=\abs{Gu-Hv}^{2}$,
$u^{\top}\Sigma_{uu}^{-1}u/\nu=\abs{G_0u}^{2}$ and
$v^{\top}\Sigma_{vv}^{-1}v/\nu=\abs{H_0v}^{2}$, and
$\log r=\log p(u,v)-\log p(u)-\log p(v)$ gives \eqref{eq:logquad}. The family
\eqref{eq:logquadfamily} contains $\log r+\mathrm{const}$, so it is
well-specified for every loss by \Cref{prop:target}. For the log-decay rate,
let $g,g_0$ be linear and injective. Then
$\log(1+\abs{f(u)-g(t\theta')}^{2})=2\log t+\log\abs{g(\theta)}^{2}+o(1)$ and
$\log(1+\abs{g_0(t\theta')}^{2})=2\log t+\log\abs{g_0(\theta)}^{2}+o(1)$, so
$s(u,t\theta')+(b-b_v)\log t$ converges to a finite continuous limit, and
\ref{R1} holds with $\beta=b-b_v$ for the same reason.
\qed

\noindent\emph{Proof of \Cref{prop:clip}.}
\label{app:clip}
Write $p=\mu_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$, so that $\Dd p/\Dd\mu_v=r\define r(u,\cdot)$,
which is positive $\mu_v$-almost everywhere by \Cref{ass:data}\ref{ass:joint}.
The function
$\Phi(\lambda)\define\int\min\{\max\{r/\lambda,m\},M\}\,\Dd\mu_v$ is continuous
and non-increasing on $(0,\infty)$, with limits $M\ge1$ as $\lambda\to0$ and
$m\le1$ as $\lambda\to\infty$, so $\Phi(\lambda)=1$ for some $\lambda$; this
defines $\rho^{\star}$. Since $m\le\rho,\rho^{\star}\le M$ and
$\KL{p}{\mu_v}<\infty$, both divergences are finite and
\begin{equation*}
 \KL{p}{\rho\,\mu_v}-\KL{p}{\rho^{\star}\mu_v}
 =\int r\log\frac{\rho^{\star}}{\rho}\,\Dd\mu_v
 \ge\int\frac{r}{\rho^{\star}}\bigl(\rho^{\star}-\rho\bigr)\,\Dd\mu_v,
\end{equation*}
using $\log x\ge1-1/x$, with equality only at $x=1$. We claim that
$\frac{r}{\rho^{\star}}(\rho^{\star}-\rho)\ge\lambda(\rho^{\star}-\rho)$
pointwise. Where $m<\rho^{\star}<M$, $r/\rho^{\star}=\lambda$. Where
$\rho^{\star}=M$, we have $r/\lambda\ge M$, so $r/\rho^{\star}\ge\lambda$,
and $\rho^{\star}-\rho=M-\rho\ge0$. Where $\rho^{\star}=m$, we have
$r/\lambda\le m$, so $r/\rho^{\star}\le\lambda$, and
$\rho^{\star}-\rho=m-\rho\le0$. Hence the right-hand side is at least
$\lambda\int(\rho^{\star}-\rho)\,\Dd\mu_v=0$, with equality only if
$\rho=\rho^{\star}$ almost everywhere. For the statement about scores, a
score with oscillation at most $2\kappa$ has a tilting factor with
$\sup\rho\le e^{2\kappa}\inf\rho$; apply the first part with $m=\inf\rho$
and $M=e^{2\kappa}m$, and take logarithms of \eqref{eq:clip}, with
$c(u)=-\log\lambda$ and $a(u)=\log m$.
\qed

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Proof of \Cref{cor:floor}.}
If $r(u,v)\to0$ as $\abs{v}\to\infty$, then $r/\lambda<m$ outside a bounded
set, where \eqref{eq:clip} gives $\rho^{\star}=m$. The score
$\log\rho^{\star}$ is then constant outside a bounded set, so \ref{R1} and
\ref{R2} hold with $\beta=0$ and $\ell\equiv\log m$, and its normalizing
constant is one.
\qed

\noindent\emph{Proof of \Cref{prop:linearsat}.}
\label{app:linearsat}
Write $v=R\,\Sigma_{vv}^{1/2}U$ with $U$ on the unit sphere, independent of
$R$ and symmetric, $U\overset{d}{=}-U$. (i) With $g$ linear,
$g(v)/\abs{g(v)}$ depends on $v$ only through $U$, so $e^{s(u,v)}=\phi(U)$
for some function $\phi$, and by independence
$\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(R>t\vert u)=\E[\phi(U)\indicator\{R>t\}]/\E[\phi(U)]=\mathbb{P}(R>t)$.
(ii) Both $g$ and $\tanh g$ are odd, so $s(u,-v)=-s(u,v)$. Since $\mu_v$ is
symmetric, $\int_Be^{s}\,\Dd\mu_v=\int_{-B}e^{-s}\,\Dd\mu_v$, and for $B=-B$
this gives $\int_Be^{s}\,\Dd\mu_v=\int_B\cosh s\,\Dd\mu_v$; apply it to $B$
and to $B=\VV$. For the last claim, condition on $U$: the function
$r\mapsto\cosh s(u,r\Sigma_{vv}^{1/2}U)$ is non-decreasing because $\cosh$ is
even and increasing on $[0,\infty)$, and so is $r\mapsto\indicator\{r>t\}$.
Two non-decreasing functions of the same random variable $R$ are
non-negatively correlated, so
$\E[\cosh s\;\indicator\{R>t\}\mid U]\ge\E[\cosh s\mid U]\,\mathbb{P}(R>t)$;
integrate over $U$ and divide by $\E\cosh s$. For the inner product,
$\abs{s(u,rv)}=r\abs{s(u,v)}$; for $d_z=1$,
$\abs{s(u,rv)}=\kappa\abs{f(u)}\tanh(r\abs{g(v)})$.
\qed

\subsection{Proof of \texorpdfstring{\Cref{thm:exposure}}{Theorem 4.21}}
\label{app:exposure}
Write $\rho_j\define\rho(v_j)$, so that
$\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)=\sum_{j:v_j\in T}\rho_j\big/\sum_{k}\rho_k$ (the
factor $Z(u)$ cancels), and let $K\define\#\{j:v_j\in T\}$, which is binomial
with parameters $N$ and $\pi$.

(i) $\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)\le\indicator\{K\ge1\}$ for any weights, and
$\mathbb{P}(K\ge1)=P_N\le N\pi$.

(ii) Condition on the set $J$ of indices with $v_j\in T$, with $\abs{J}=K\ge1$.
Let $S_T\define\sum_{j\in J}\rho_j\ge Ka$ and
$S_c\define\sum_{j\notin J}\rho_j$. Given $J$, the futures with $j\notin J$
are independent draws from $\mu_v(\,\cdot\mid T^{c})$, so
\begin{equation*}
 \E[S_c\mid J]=(N-K)\,\frac{\E_{\mu_v}[\rho\,\indicator_{T^{c}}]}{1-\pi}
 =(N-K)\,\frac{1-\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)}{1-\pi}\le N-K,
\end{equation*}
because $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)=\E_{\mu_v}[\rho\indicator_T]\ge a\pi\ge\pi$.
Since $x\mapsto x/(x+S_c)$ is increasing and $x\mapsto Ka/(Ka+x)$ is convex,
Jensen's inequality gives
\begin{equation}
 \E\bigl[\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)\,\big\vert\,J\bigr]
 \ge\E\Bigl[\frac{Ka}{Ka+S_c}\,\Big\vert\,J\Bigr]
 \ge\frac{Ka}{Ka+N-K}\ge\frac{a}{a+N-1},
 \label{eq:condweight}
\end{equation}
the last step because $K\mapsto Ka/(K(a-1)+N)$ is increasing and $K\ge1$.
Taking expectations over $J$ on the event $\{K\ge1\}$ gives \eqref{eq:growth}.

(iii) By exchangeability and with $S'\define\sum_{k\ge2}\rho_k$, which is
independent of $v_1$,
\begin{equation*}
 \E\,\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)=N\,\E\Bigl[\frac{\rho_1\indicator_T(v_1)}{\rho_1+S'}\Bigr]
 \le N\pi\,\E\Bigl[\frac{b}{b+S'}\Bigr],
\end{equation*}
using that $x\mapsto x/(x+S')$ is increasing and $\rho_1\le b$ on $T$. The
variables $\rho_k$ are independent with mean $1$ and variance $\chi^{2}$, so
Chebyshev's inequality gives
$\mathbb{P}\bigl(S'<\tfrac{N-1}{2}\bigr)\le4\chi^{2}/(N-1)$, and therefore
$\E[b/(b+S')]\le4\chi^{2}/(N-1)+2b/(N-1)$. Hence
$\E\,\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)\le\frac{N}{N-1}(2b+4\chi^{2})\pi\le(4b+8\chi^{2})\pi$
for $N\ge2$. The almost sure limit follows from the strong law of large
numbers applied to $\frac1N\sum_j\rho_j\indicator_T(v_j)$ and
$\frac1N\sum_j\rho_j$, and it lies in $[a\pi,b\pi]$ because $a\le\rho\le b$ on
$T$.

For \eqref{eq:minlaw}: if $N\pi\le1$ then $P_N\ge N\pi-\tfrac12(N\pi)^{2}\ge\tfrac12N\pi$,
and $a/(a+N-1)\ge\tfrac12\min(1,a/N)$, so \eqref{eq:growth} gives
$\mathrm{OF}_N(T)\ge\tfrac14\min(N,a)$. The upper bound combines (i) and
(iii).
\qed

\subsection{Proof of \texorpdfstring{\Cref{lem:energytail}}{Lemma 4.22}}
\label{app:energytail}
Let $k(x,y)\define\abs{x}+\abs{y}-\abs{x-y}$. Because the Euclidean distance
is of negative type, $k$ is a positive semidefinite kernel, the
distance-induced kernel centred at the origin \cite{sejdinovic2013equivalence};
it is twice the covariance of L\'evy's Brownian motion. For finite signed measures $m,m'$
with finite first moments, $\langle m,m'\rangle_k\define\iint k\,\Dd m\,\Dd m'$
is therefore a positive semidefinite bilinear form, and the Cauchy--Schwarz
inequality holds for it. Let $m\define q-p$. Since $m$ has total mass zero,
the terms $\abs{x}+\abs{y}$ integrate to zero and
\begin{equation*}
 \langle m,m\rangle_k=-\iint\abs{x-y}\,m(\Dd x)\,m(\Dd y)
 =2\,\E\abs{X-Y}-\E\abs{X-X'}-\E\abs{Y-Y'}=\DE(p,q).
\end{equation*}
Let $z\define te$ and $\varphi\define k(\cdot,z)$. The Cauchy--Schwarz
inequality with $m'=\delta_z$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \Bigl(\int\varphi\,\Dd q-\int\varphi\,\Dd p\Bigr)^{2}
 =\langle\delta_z,m\rangle_k^{2}\le k(z,z)\,\langle m,m\rangle_k=2t\,\DE(p,q).
 \label{eq:witness}
\end{equation}
By the triangle inequality, $0\le\varphi(x)\le2\abs{x}$ for every $x$, so
$\int\varphi\,\Dd p\le2\E_p\abs{X}$. For $x\in H_{t,e}$ we have
$\abs{x-z}^{2}=\abs{x}^{2}+t^{2}-2t\IP{x}{e}\le\abs{x}^{2}+t^{2}$, hence
$\varphi(x)\ge\abs{x}+t-\sqrt{\abs{x}^{2}+t^{2}}$. The right-hand side is
increasing in $\abs{x}$, and equals $(2-\sqrt2)t$ at $\abs{x}=t$, so
$\varphi\ge c_0t$ on $H_{t,e}$ and $\int\varphi\,\Dd q\ge c_0t\,q(H_{t,e})$.
Inserting both bounds into \eqref{eq:witness} gives \eqref{eq:energytail}.
\qed

\subsection{Proofs of \texorpdfstring{\Cref{thm:energycost}}{Theorem 4.23} and \texorpdfstring{\Cref{cor:collapsecost}}{Corollary 4.24}}
\label{app:energycost}
\emph{Proof of \Cref{thm:energycost}.}
For a probability measure $q$ with a finite first moment and $y\sim p$,
$\E_y\mathrm{ES}(q,y)=\E\abs{X-Y}-\tfrac12\E\abs{X-X'}$ with $X,X'\sim q$ and
$Y\sim p$, and $\E_y\mathrm{ES}(p,y)=\tfrac12\E\abs{Y-Y'}$. The difference is
$\tfrac12\DE(p,q)$. Applying this to $q=\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}$, which has finite
support, and averaging over the bank gives
$\mathcal{E}_N(u)=\tfrac12\E\,\DE(p,\nu^{N}_{v\vert u})$. Let $K$ be the
number of bank futures in $T$ and write $\omega\define\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}(T\vert u)$.
Since $T\subseteq H_{t,e}$, \Cref{lem:energytail} gives
$\DE(p,\nu^{N}_{v\vert u})\ge\psi(\omega)$ with
$\psi(x)\define\frac{1}{2t}(c_0tx-2m_p)_{+}^{2}$, which is convex and
increasing. Hence, by Jensen's inequality and \eqref{eq:condweight},
\begin{equation*}
 \E\,\DE(p,\nu^{N}_{v\vert u})\ge\mathbb{P}(K\ge1)\,\E\bigl[\psi(\omega)\mid K\ge1\bigr]
 \ge P_N\,\psi\bigl(\E[\omega\mid K\ge1]\bigr)\ge P_N\,\psi\Bigl(\frac{a}{a+N-1}\Bigr),
\end{equation*}
which is \eqref{eq:energycost} after multiplying by $\tfrac12$. If
$N\le\min(a,1/\pi)$ and $c_0t\ge8m_p$, then $a/(a+N-1)\ge\tfrac12$ and
$P_N\ge\tfrac12N\pi$, so
$\mathcal{E}_N(u)\ge\frac{N\pi}{8t}\bigl(\tfrac12c_0t-\tfrac14c_0t\bigr)^{2}=\frac{c_0^{2}}{128}N\pi t$.
The bound for the limit is \Cref{lem:energytail} with $q=\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}$,
understood as $+\infty$ if $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}$ has no first moment.
\qed

\noindent\emph{Proof of \Cref{cor:collapsecost}.}
The scores $X_j=\IP{a(u)}{v_j}$ are scaled univariate $t$ variables, so
\Cref{prop:collapse} applies with $\alpha=\nu$ and $b_N\asymp N^{1/\nu}$. Let
$j^{\star}$ be the index of the largest score and $w^{\star}$ its weight. By
the proof of \Cref{prop:collapse}, $w^{\star}\to1$ in probability and
$X_{j^{\star}}/b_N$ converges in distribution to a positive random variable,
and $\abs{v_{j^{\star}}}\ge X_{j^{\star}}/\abs{a(u)}$. Apply
\Cref{lem:energytail} with $p=\delta_y$, $q=\nu^{N}_{v\vert u}$,
$t=\abs{v_{j^{\star}}}$ and $e=v_{j^{\star}}/t$, so that $v_{j^{\star}}\in H_{t,e}$
and $q(H_{t,e})\ge w^{\star}$. Since
$\mathrm{ES}(q,y)=\tfrac12\DE(\delta_y,q)$,
\begin{equation*}
 \mathrm{ES}(\nu^{N}_{v\vert u},y)\ge\frac{1}{4t}\bigl(c_0t\,w^{\star}-2\abs{y}\bigr)_{+}^{2}
 \ge\frac{c_0^{2}}{64}\,\abs{v_{j^{\star}}}
 \qquad\text{on }\bigl\{w^{\star}\ge\tfrac12,\ c_0\abs{v_{j^{\star}}}\ge8\abs{y}\bigr\}.
\end{equation*}
The probability of this event tends to one, and for every $\epsilon>0$ there
is $c>0$ with $\liminf_N\mathbb{P}(\abs{v_{j^{\star}}}\ge cN^{1/\nu})\ge1-\epsilon$.
\qed

\subsection{Proof of \texorpdfstring{\Cref{prop:klvsenergy}}{Proposition 4.25}}
\label{app:klvsenergy}
(i) The model $q_h$ has density
$e^{-h\indicator_T}/\bigl(1-(1-e^{-h})\varepsilon\bigr)$ with respect to $q$,
where the denominator is positive because $\varepsilon<1$. Hence
\begin{equation*}
 \KL{p}{q_h}=\KL{p}{q}+h\,p(T)+\log\bigl(1-(1-e^{-h})\varepsilon\bigr)\eqqcolon\KL{p}{q}+\phi(h).
\end{equation*}
The function $\phi$ is convex, and
$\phi'(h)=p(T)-e^{-h}\varepsilon/(1-(1-e^{-h})\varepsilon)=p(T)-q_h(T)$. If
$p(T)>0$, the minimum is attained where $q_h(T)=p(T)$, that is, at
$e^{-h}=p(T)(1-\varepsilon)/(\varepsilon(1-p(T)))$, and substituting gives
$\phi(h)=-p(T)\log\frac{p(T)}{\varepsilon}-(1-p(T))\log\frac{1-p(T)}{1-\varepsilon}$.
If $p(T)=0$, $\phi$ decreases to $\log(1-\varepsilon)$ as $h\to\infty$, which
is the same formula with $0\log0=0$. For the bound, $p(T)\le\varepsilon$
gives $p(T)\log\frac{p(T)}{\varepsilon}\le0$ and
$(1-p(T))\log\frac{1-p(T)}{1-\varepsilon}\le-\log(1-\varepsilon)$. (ii) is

\subsection{Proof of \texorpdfstring{\Cref{prop:bankenergy}}{Proposition 4.27}}
\label{app:bankenergy}
(i) Condition on $u$ and on the bank, and apply the identity
$\E_y\mathrm{ES}(q,y)-\E_y\mathrm{ES}(p,y)=\tfrac12\DE(p,q)$ from the proof of
\Cref{thm:energycost} with $p=\mu_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$ and

(ii) Let $\abs{s}\le M$, so that $e^{-2M}\le\rho\le e^{2M}$ and the bank

$\sum_jw_j\abs{v_j-y}\to\E_q\abs{X-y}$ almost surely, and by the strong law
for $U$-statistics \cite{hoeffding1961strong}, whose kernel
$\rho(x)\rho(x')\abs{x-x'}$ is integrable,
$\sum_{j,k}w_jw_k\abs{v_j-v_k}\to\E_q\abs{X-X'}$ almost surely. Hence

almost surely to $e^{4M}\E_{\mu_v}\abs{V-y}$, and their expectations over the

functions), $J^{\mathrm{E}}_{K}(s)\to\E_{(u,y)\sim\mu}\,\mathrm{ES}(\nu^{s}_{v\vert u},y)$,

\subsection{Proofs for \texorpdfstring{\Cref{ssec:dimension}}{Section 4.4}}
\label{app:dimension}
\emph{Proof of \Cref{prop:infosplit}.}
Mutual information is invariant under invertible linear maps of $u$ and of
$v$, so we may pass to the canonical coordinates $(a,b)$, whose scale matrix
$\tilde\Sigma$ has identity diagonal blocks and off-diagonal block
$\mathrm{diag}(\rho)$; thus $\det\tilde\Sigma=\prod_i(1-\rho_i^{2})$. Let
$D=d_u+d_v$. The density of a $D$-dimensional Student-$t$ vector $z$ with
scale matrix $\tilde\Sigma$ is
$\Gamma(\tfrac{\nu+D}{2})\Gamma(\tfrac\nu2)^{-1}(\nu\pi)^{-D/2}(\det\tilde\Sigma)^{-1/2}(1+q/\nu)^{-(\nu+D)/2}$
with $q=z^{\top}\tilde\Sigma^{-1}z$, and the marginals of $a$ and $b$ are
standard Student-$t$ with the same $\nu$ \cite{kotz2004multivariate}. Hence
\begin{align*}
 \log r & =\log\frac{\Gamma(\frac{\nu+D}{2})\Gamma(\frac\nu2)}{\Gamma(\frac{\nu+d_u}{2})\Gamma(\frac{\nu+d_v}{2})}
 -\frac12\sum_i\log(1-\rho_i^{2})                                                                                  \\
        & \quad-\frac{\nu+D}{2}\log\Bigl(1+\frac q\nu\Bigr)
 +\frac{\nu+d_u}{2}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{\abs{a}^{2}}{\nu}\Bigr)
 +\frac{\nu+d_v}{2}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{\abs{b}^{2}}{\nu}\Bigr).
\end{align*}
Writing $z=\tilde\Sigma^{1/2}\xi/\sqrt{w}$ with $\xi$ standard Gaussian and
$\nu w\sim\chi^{2}_{\nu}$ independent, $1+q/\nu=(\chi^{2}_{\nu}+\chi^{2}_{D})/\chi^{2}_{\nu}$
with independent chi-squared variables, whatever $\tilde\Sigma$ is, and
$\E\log\chi^{2}_{n}=\psi(n/2)+\log2$. Therefore
$\E\log(1+q/\nu)=\psi(\tfrac{\nu+D}{2})-\psi(\tfrac\nu2)$, and likewise for
$\abs{a}^{2}$ and $\abs{b}^{2}$ with $d_u$ and $d_v$ in place of $D$. Taking
expectations gives \eqref{eq:infosplit}. Setting $\rho=0$ shows that
$c_\nu(m,n)$ is the mutual information of the two blocks of an uncorrelated
Student-$t$ vector, which is not a product measure, so $c_\nu(m,n)>0$. For
$n'<n$, let $v'$ consist of the first $n'$ coordinates of $v$ and $v''$ of
the others; by the chain rule
$c_\nu(m,n)-c_\nu(m,n')=I(u;v''\vert v')$, which is positive because the
conditional scale of $v''$ given $(u,v')$ depends on $\abs{u}$. For
\eqref{eq:cscaleasym}, write $x_j=(\nu+j)/2$ and insert Stirling's formula
$\log\Gamma(x)=(x-\tfrac12)\log x-x+\tfrac12\log2\pi+O(1/x)$ and
$\psi(x)=\log x-\tfrac1{2x}+O(x^{-2})$ into \eqref{eq:cscale}: the terms of
order $x\log x$ cancel, the terms linear in $x$ combine through
$x_m+x_n-x_{m+n}=\nu/2$, and what remains is
$\tfrac12\log\bigl(x_mx_n/x_{m+n}\bigr)+\tfrac\nu2-\tfrac\nu2\psi(\tfrac\nu2)+\log\Gamma(\tfrac\nu2)-\tfrac12\log2\pi-\tfrac12+o(1)$,
with $x_mx_n/x_{m+n}=\tfrac{mn}{2(m+n)}(1+o(1))$.
\qed

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Proof of \Cref{thm:bottleneck}.}
(i) For bounded measurable $\phi$, conditioning on $h(v)$ under $\mu_v$,
\begin{equation*}
 \int\phi(v)\,\frac{e^{S(u,h(v))}}{Z(u)}\,\mu_v(\Dd v)
 =\int\E_{\mu_v}\bigl[\phi(v)\,\big\vert\,h(v)=w\bigr]\,\frac{e^{S(u,w)}}{Z(u)}\,\mu_h(\Dd w).
\end{equation*}
(ii) Fix $u$ and write $p=\mu_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$ and
$q=\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$, with $p_h,q_h$ the laws of $h(v)$. By
the chain rule for the KL divergence and part (i),
\begin{equation*}
 \KL{p}{q}=\KL{p_h}{\pi_u}+\E_{w\sim p_h}\KL{p(\cdot\vert h=w)}{\mu_v(\cdot\vert h=w)}.
\end{equation*}
The expectation over $u\sim\mu_u$ of the second term is the conditional
mutual information $I(u;v\vert h(v))$, which equals $I(u;v)-I(u;h(v))$
because $h(v)$ is a function of $v$; it is finite by \ref{ass:MI}. The first
term is non-negative and vanishes if and only if $\pi_u$ is the law of
$h(v)$ given $u$, that is, $e^{S(u,w)}/Z(u)=r_h(u,w)$.
(iii) The model depends on $u$ through $g(u)$ only; write it as $m_{g(u)}$,
and let $\bar p_{g(u)}$ be the law of $v$ given $g(u)$, the average of
$\mu_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$ over $u$ given $g(u)$. Then
\begin{equation*}
 \Jvu^{\mathrm{KL}}(s)=\E_{\mu_u}\KL{\mu_{v\vert u}}{\bar p_{g(u)}}+\E_{\mu_u}\KL{\bar p_{g(u)}}{m_{g(u)}}.
\end{equation*}
The first term is $I(u;v\vert g(u))=I(u;v)-I(g(u);v)$, and the second is at
least $I(g(u);v)-I(g(u);h(v))$ by part (ii) applied to the pair $(g(u),v)$.
\qed

\noindent\emph{Proof of \Cref{cor:tdimension}.}
(i) We may assume that $H$ has rank $d_z$; a smaller rank is a smaller
$d_z$, and the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:dimfloor} is non-increasing in
$d_z$. The pair $(u,Hv)$ is again elliptical Student-$t$ with $\nu$ degrees
of freedom, of dimensions $d_u$ and $d_z$, so by \Cref{prop:infosplit}
$I(u;Hv)=-\tfrac12\sum_i\log(1-\rho_i(H)^{2})+c_\nu(d_u,d_z)$, where
$\rho_i(H)$ are the canonical correlations of $u$ and $Hv$. In canonical
coordinates $Hv=\tilde H b$, and the whitened form of $\tilde Hb$ is $Pb$ with
$P\in\RR^{d_z\times d_v}$, $PP^{\top}=I$; the $\rho_i(H)$ are the singular
values of $\mathrm{diag}(\rho)P^{\top}$, which satisfy
$\rho_i(H)\le\rho_i$ for $i\le d_z$, with equality when $P$ selects the first
$d_z$ coordinates. Inserting this into \Cref{thm:bottleneck}(ii) and using
\eqref{eq:infosplit} gives \eqref{eq:dimfloor} and the case of equality. The
second term is positive for $d_z<d_v$ by the monotonicity of $c_\nu$. With a
linear past encoder the same argument applies to the pair $(Gu,Hv)$ and
\Cref{thm:bottleneck}(iii): its canonical correlations are the singular
values of $P'\,\mathrm{diag}(\rho)P^{\top}$ with $P'P'^{\top}=I$, again
dominated by the $\rho_i$, and its scale term is $c_\nu(d_z,d_z)$.
(ii) In the notation of the proof of \Cref{prop:infosplit}, completing the
square in each canonical pair gives
\begin{equation*}
 q=\abs{a}^{2}+\abs{b}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl[\frac{(b_i-\rho_ia_i)^{2}}{1-\rho_i^{2}}-b_i^{2}\Bigr],
\end{equation*}
so $\log r$ depends on $v$ only through $b_1,\dots,b_k$ and $\abs{b}^{2}$.
Then $r(u,v)=r_h(u,h(v))$ and $I(u;h(v))=\E_\mu\log r_h=I(u;v)$.
\qed

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Proof of \Cref{thm:tailcap}.}
Choose an orthogonal matrix $Q$ whose first $d_z$ rows span the row space of
$H\Sigma_{vv}^{1/2}$, and write $Q\Sigma_{vv}^{-1/2}v=(w,y)$ with
$w\in\RR^{d_z}$, $y\in\RR^{d_v-d_z}$. Then $Hv$ is an invertible linear
function of $w$, so $s$ depends on $v$ through $w$ only, and under $\mu_v$
the vector $(w,y)$ is standard Student-$t$ with $\nu$ degrees of freedom. The
conditional law of $y$ given $w$ is Student-$t$ with $\nu+d_z$ degrees of
freedom, location $0$ and scale matrix $\frac{\nu+\abs{w}^{2}}{\nu+d_z}I$
\cite{kotz2004multivariate}. Hence
$T\define y\,\sqrt{(\nu+d_z)/(\nu+\abs{w}^{2})}$ is, given $w$, a standard
Student-$t$ vector with $\nu+d_z$ degrees of freedom, for every $w$. By
\Cref{thm:bottleneck}(i) the conditional law of $y$ given $w$ is the same
under $\nu^{s}_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$, so $T$ has this law under the model
as well, for every $u$. Finally
\begin{equation*}
 \abs{v}^{2}=(w,y)^{\top}Q\Sigma_{vv}Q^{\top}(w,y)\ge\lambda\abs{y}^{2}
 =\lambda\,\frac{\nu+\abs{w}^{2}}{\nu+d_z}\,\abs{T}^{2}\ge\frac{\lambda\nu}{\nu+d_z}\,\abs{T}^{2},
\end{equation*}
which gives the inequality in \eqref{eq:tailcap}; the asymptotic
equivalence is the regular variation of the Student-$t$ distribution. By
\Cref{lem:tprocess},
$\mu_{v\vert u}(\abs{v}>t\vert u)\sim c(u)\,t^{-(\nu+d_u)}$, which gives the
bound on $\varrho_t(u)$.
\qed

\smallskip
\noindent\emph{Proof of \Cref{prop:odd}.}
The symmetry of the conditionals implies that of $\mu_v$. The term
$\alpha(u)$ cancels in $s-\log Z(u)$, so let $\alpha=0$. Then
\begin{equation*}
 \Jvu^{\mathrm{KL}}(s)=\E_\mu\bigl[\log r-s\bigr]+\E_{\mu_u}\log Z(u)
 =I(u;v)-\E_{\mu_v}\gamma-\E_\mu o+\E_{\mu_u}\log Z(u).
\end{equation*}
Since $o(u,\cdot)$ is odd and $\mu_{v\vert u}(\cdot\vert u)$ symmetric,
$\E_\mu o=0$. Since $\gamma$ is even, $o(u,\cdot)$ odd and $\mu_v$ symmetric,
$Z(u)=\E_{\mu_v}\bigl[e^{\gamma}\cosh o(u,\cdot)\bigr]\ge Z_\gamma$, with
equality if and only if $o(u,\cdot)=0$ $\mu_v$-almost everywhere. Therefore
$\Jvu^{\mathrm{KL}}(s)\ge I(u;v)-\E_{\mu_v}\gamma+\log Z_\gamma=I(u;v)+\KL{\mu_v}{e^{\gamma}\mu_v/Z_\gamma}$,
and the last term vanishes if and only if $\gamma$ is constant.
\qed
