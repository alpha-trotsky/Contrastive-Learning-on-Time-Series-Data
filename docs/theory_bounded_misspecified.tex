\title{For flexible encoders, neither the contrastive loss\\ nor the divergence matters;
the score matters\\ only through its tails}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=blue!3!white,colframe=blue!40!black,title=Thesis]
For flexible encoders and infinite data, neither the choice of contrastive loss (one-way,
two-way or joint) nor the choice of divergence it minimises (KL, as in CLIP, or an
alternative such as an energy distance) matters: every combination aims at the same target
and can reach it. What still matters is the choice of \emph{score}, and it matters only
through the score's \emph{tail behaviour}:
\begin{itemize}
\item a score that is \textbf{bounded above} (\Ltwo{}, normalised cosine) defines a valid
  model for every data distribution, and the forecast built from a bank of real futures
  converges to that model as the bank grows;
\item a score that \textbf{grows without bound} in the future (the unnormalised dot product)
  has no valid model on heavy-tailed data, and its bank forecast degenerates as the bank
  grows, putting all its weight on a single future.
\end{itemize}
Experiments confirm the tail half, with one refinement: a ceiling is necessary but not
sufficient in practice. Training never sees the far tails, and KL barely charges for
misplaced tail mass, so the score must also not \emph{reward} extreme futures, which
\Ltwo{} and normalised cosine do not. The loss half is confirmed for the loss variants; the
divergence half is proved but not yet tested, since all our models were trained with KL.
\end{tcolorbox}

\noindent
``Valid model'' has a precise meaning here: the normalising constant of the model's forecast,
$Z(u)$ below, is finite, so the forecast is a probability distribution and the loss has a
finite value. When it is infinite, the object the loss is trying to learn does not exist.

\medskip\noindent
The note builds the thesis in four steps. Section~\ref{sec:paper} sets out the framework of
Baptista, Stuart \& Tran~\cite{bst} that everything rests on. Section~\ref{sec:evidence}
collects what three rounds of experiments showed when we took that framework from its
Gaussian, linear setting to time series. Section~\ref{sec:loss} proves the first half of the
thesis (neither the loss nor the divergence matters) and Section~\ref{sec:tails} the second
(the tails decide).
Section~\ref{sec:exp} reports two new experiments that test the second half directly.
Section~\ref{sec:finite} explains what changes with finite data, and
Section~\ref{sec:lit} places the results in the literature.

\section{The framework: a contrastive model is a forecast}\label{sec:paper}

\paragraph{Data and score.} The past is $u$, the future is $v$, the data distribution is
$\mu(du,dv)$, and its marginals are $\mu_u,\mu_v$. We assume $\mu$ and $\mu_u\otimes\mu_v$ are
mutually absolutely continuous and write
\[
  r(u,v) = \frac{d\mu}{d(\mu_u\otimes\mu_v)}(u,v) = \frac{p(v\mid u)}{p(v)} .
\]
So $\log r$ is the pointwise mutual information: how much more likely this future is
given this past than it is in general. Its average is the mutual information
$I(u;v)=\E_\mu\log r$.

A contrastive model is a \emph{score} $s(u,v)$, built from a past encoder $f$, a future
encoder $g$ and a scale $\kappa>0$ (the learned logit scale). We use three scores:
\[
  s_{\rm dot} = \kappa\,\langle f(u),g(v)\rangle,\qquad
  s_{\rm ncos} = \kappa\,\Big\langle \tfrac{f(u)}{|f(u)|},\tfrac{g(v)}{|g(v)|}\Big\rangle,\qquad
  s_{\Ltwo} = -\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\,|f(u)-g(v)|^2 .
\]
$s_{\rm dot}$ is the paper's unnormalised ``cosine'' score, $s_{\rm ncos}$ is real CLIP's
normalised cosine, and $s_{\Ltwo}$ is the paper's \Ltwo{} score. Note that $s_{\Ltwo}\le 0$
and $|s_{\rm ncos}|\le\kappa$, while $s_{\rm dot}$ is unbounded whenever $g$ is.

\paragraph{The model.} The paper's central observation (its eqs.~(13)--(16)) is that a
score defines a probability model by tilting the product of the marginals:
\[
  \nu(du,dv) = \frac{e^{s(u,v)}}{Z}\,\mu_u(du)\,\mu_v(dv),\qquad
  \nu_\vu(dv\mid u) = \frac{e^{s(u,v)}}{Z(u)}\,\mu_v(dv),
\]
\[
  Z = \iint e^{s}\,d\mu_u\,d\mu_v,\qquad Z(u) = \int e^{s(u,v)}\,\mu_v(dv).
\]
In words: the forecast for a past $u$ takes the distribution of all futures and reweights
each one by $e^{\text{score}}$.

\paragraph{The losses.} The CLIP-style losses are, up to constants, divergences between the
data and this model (the paper's Thm~3.2, Thm~3.8 and Remark~3.12):
\begin{align*}
  J_\vu(s)   &= \E_{u}\,\KL\big(\mu_\vu(\cdot\mid u)\,\|\,\nu_\vu(\cdot\mid u)\big)
               && \text{one-way (the forecasting direction)},\\
  J_{2}(s)   &= \lambda_u J_\vu(s) + \lambda_v J_\uv(s),\quad \lambda_u,\lambda_v>0
               && \text{two-way (standard CLIP)},\\
  J_{\rm joint}(s) &= \KL(\mu\,\|\,\nu)
               && \text{joint (one softmax over all pairs)}.
\end{align*}

\paragraph{Other divergences.} The paper also replaces KL by a general divergence $D$
(its Optimisation Problems~3.4 and~3.6), giving losses $J^D_\vu(s)=\E_u D(\mu_\vu,\nu_\vu)$,
$J^D_2=\lambda_uJ^D_\vu+\lambda_vJ^D_\uv$ and $J^D_{\rm joint}=D(\mu,\nu)$. Its Remark~3.5 notes that
such a loss can be trained from samples alone when $D$ is the KL divergence, and also when
$D$ is an energy distance or a maximum mean discrepancy (MMD). Throughout, a \emph{divergence}
is any $D$ with $D(p,q)\ge0$ and $D(p,q)=0$ only if $p=q$. The energy distance (on distributions
with a finite mean) and MMD with a characteristic kernel both qualify. The energy distance
is also exactly what our evaluation measures: the energy score of a forecast $q$, minus that
of the truth $p$, is $\frac12 D_E(p,q)$ with
$D_E(p,q)=2\E|X-Y|-\E|X-X'|-\E|Y-Y'|$, where $X,X'\sim p$ and $Y,Y'\sim q$ are independent.

\paragraph{The forecast we actually compute.} The paper's eq.~(31) turns the model into a
forecast using a finite \emph{bank} $v_1,\dots,v_N$ of real futures:
\[
  \nu^N_\vu(\cdot\mid u) = \sum_{j=1}^N w_j\,\delta_{v_j},\qquad
  w_j = \frac{e^{s(u,v_j)}}{\sum_k e^{s(u,v_k)}},\qquad
  \mathrm{ESS} = \Big(\sum_j w_j^2\Big)^{-1}.
\]
This is self-normalised importance sampling with proposal $\mu_v$. ESS, the effective
sample size, counts how many bank futures the forecast really uses. ESS $\approx1$ means the
forecast has collapsed onto a single future.

\paragraph{The paper's predictions.} With Gaussian data and linear encoders, the paper
derives every minimiser in closed form. Four predictions matter here:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}lL@{}}
\toprule
P1 & The dot product gets the forecast's average right but its spread wrong: the spread is
     stuck at ``knowing nothing'' (Cor.~5.2). \\
P2 & \Ltwo{} trained one-way gets both average and spread right (Thm~5.3, Cor.~5.4). \\
P3 & For the dot product, one-way and two-way give the same answer (Thm~5.1). \\
P4 & The joint loss shrinks the forecast towards the average, so it is a worse forecaster
     than the one-way loss (Thm~5.6, Cor.~5.7). \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\section{What the experiments showed}\label{sec:evidence}

We tested these predictions in three rounds, relaxing one of the paper's assumptions at a
time.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Every contrastive model is scored only through its own score, by
building $\nu^N_\vu$ from a bank of real futures that were not used in training. Forecasts are
scored with the \emph{energy score}~\cite{gneiting}, a proper scoring rule for
multivariate probabilistic forecasts that rewards both accuracy and honest uncertainty. On
synthetic data each domain has an exact ``perfect forecaster'' (oracle), and we report the
energy score as a percentage above the oracle's (0\% = perfect). On real data we report
\emph{skill}, the fractional reduction in energy score against a reference forecast (higher
is better). Each synthetic setting uses 200k training pairs, a bank of 50k held-out
futures, 500 test cases and 2 seeds, so differences under about 1\% are noise. Past and
future are windows of 50 points of a smooth Gaussian process observed with noise.

\paragraph{Domains.} Each synthetic domain has a dial whose zero point is the Gaussian
process, which is the paper's setting:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{heavy tails}: an elliptical Student-$t$ process with $\nu$ degrees of freedom
  ($\nu=\infty$ is the Gaussian process, $\nu=3$ the heaviest we used);
\item \textbf{bent GP}: a monotone warp of the Gaussian process (skewed futures, nonlinear
  relation between past and future);
\item \textbf{double-well}: a stochastic process that jumps between two levels, so the future
  can have two separate likely outcomes.
\end{itemize}
The real data are one year (2020) of 10-minute readings from the Jena weather station. We
forecast the next 6\,h of temperature from the last day of six variable groups, and the next
6\,h of rain from past pressure, wind and moisture.

\paragraph{Finding 1: with linear encoders, both the loss and the score matter.} On the
Gaussian process the dot product is $+5.6\%$ above perfect whichever direction it is trained
in (P3), and $+9.8\%$ when trained jointly (P4). Its 90\% bands catch the truth 98\% of the time
on the hardest third of cases (P1: too wide). \Ltwo{} is at $+0.0\%$ in all three
variants. Linear normalised cosine is poor everywhere ($+12$--$23\%$), because it sees the past
only through its direction and loses its level.

\paragraph{Finding 2: with MLP encoders, the loss variant mostly stops mattering.} With
two-layer MLP encoders (width 256, plus a linear shortcut), the spread between the
variants collapses:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llcccc@{}}
\toprule
domain & encoder & dot 1-way & dot 2-way & dot joint & spread \\
\midrule
Gaussian process & linear & $+5.6$ & $+5.6$ & $+9.8$ & 4.2 \\
                 & MLP    & $+2.7$ & $+1.4$ & $+0.3$ & 2.4 \\
bent GP (full warp) & linear & $+37.4$ & $+40.0$ & $+25.2$ & 14.8 \\
                    & MLP    & $+5.9$ & $+3.9$ & $+0.6$ & 5.3 \\
double-well ($\lambda=1.5$) & linear & $+1.3$ & $+2.6$ & $+2.7$ & 1.4 \\
                    & MLP    & $+0.6$ & $+0.3$ & $+0.5$ & 0.3 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
P1 also fades: the dot product's coverage on the hardest cases drops from 98\% to 89\%. The
best models (\Ltwo{} joint, normalised cosine) are within about 1\% of perfect on every
domain. The differences that remain are small and consistently favour the joint loss; we
return to them in Section~\ref{sec:finite}.

\paragraph{Finding 3: every catastrophic failure is an unbounded score on heavy tails.}
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
heavy tails, $\nu=3$ & dot 2-way & normalised cosine & \Ltwo{} joint \\
\midrule
linear & $\sim10\times$ worse than perfect & $+23.0$ & $+4.5$ \\
MLP    & $+23.3$ & $+0.9$ & $+0.7$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The linear dot product's forecasts put essentially all their weight on single bank futures
(minimum ESS $1.0$). The MLP does not rescue it: MLP dot products are still $+7$ to $+23\%$
above perfect and still collapse onto single futures for some inputs. On rain, which is
mostly zero with occasional bursts, the unnormalised dot product scored 1.4--2.4 times
worse than climatology, while \Ltwo{} and normalised cosine kept the shape ``mostly dry,
sometimes a burst''. Normalisation helps exactly where tails are heavy, and only there.

\paragraph{Finding 4: with real, limited data, the loss matters again.} On weather, MLPs
over-fit within a few hundred to a few thousand steps. There, joint \Ltwo{} clearly beat
one-way \Ltwo{} (temperature skill $+0.28$ vs $+0.15$ against persistence). Section~\ref{sec:finite}
explains why this does not contradict the thesis, which is a statement about infinite data.

\section{Why neither the loss nor the divergence matters}\label{sec:loss}

Every loss variant, under every divergence, aims at the same object, the log density ratio.
The variants differ only in which part of it they leave free, and the divergences only in
how they measure a miss.

\begin{proposition}[Common target]\label{prop:target}
Let $D$ be any divergence, and $s$ an admissible score (Section~\ref{sec:tails}). Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $J^D_\vu(s)=0 \iff s(u,v)=\log r(u,v)+c(u)$ for some function $c$;
\item $J^D_\uv(s)=0 \iff s(u,v)=\log r(u,v)+c'(v)$ for some function $c'$;
\item $J^D_2(s)=0 \iff J^D_{\rm joint}(s)=0 \iff s=\log r+\text{const}$.
\end{enumerate}
The right-hand sides do not involve $D$. With $D=\KL$ these are the CLIP-style losses
$J_\vu,J_2,J_{\rm joint}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{definition}
A score family $\cS$ is \emph{well-specified} for a loss if that loss can be driven to $0$
on $\cS$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:target}, that means $\cS$ contains some $\log r+c(u)$ for
the one-way loss, and $\log r+\text{const}$ for the two-way and joint losses, whatever the
divergence.
\end{definition}

\begin{corollary}[Agreement]\label{cor:agree}
If $\cS$ contains $\log r+\text{const}$, then for every divergence $D$, the one-way, two-way
(any $\lambda_u,\lambda_v>0$) and joint losses all have minimisers that give exactly the true
forecast, $\nu_\vu=\mu_\vu$. Neither the loss variant nor the divergence can be told apart at
the population level.
\end{corollary}

So a difference between loss variants, or between divergences, is a symptom of a score
family that cannot represent $\log r$. When the family is too rigid, every (loss,
divergence) pair picks its own closest miss. The converse fails: P3 is a case where the
family cannot represent $\log r$, yet two variants still agree.

\paragraph{Where divergences differ: misplaced tail mass.} Under misspecification the
divergences can disagree sharply, and they disagree most about the tails. The simplest case
is a forecast that is right except for a small amount of mass moved far away.

\begin{proposition}[KL is blind to how far misplaced mass goes]\label{prop:farmass}
Let $p$ be a distribution on $\R^d$ with a finite mean and no atom at $a$, and let
$q_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)p+\varepsilon\,\delta_a$. Then
\[
  \KL(p\,\|\,q_\varepsilon)\le-\log(1-\varepsilon)\quad\text{for every } a,
  \qquad
  \tfrac12 D_E(p,q_\varepsilon)=\tfrac12\varepsilon^2\big(2\,\E|X-a|-\E|X-X'|\big)\;\xrightarrow[|a|\to\infty]{}\;\infty .
\]
\end{proposition}

So moving a fraction $\varepsilon$ of the forecast to an absurdly extreme future costs at most about
$\varepsilon$ in KL, however extreme the future, while its cost in energy score grows linearly
with the distance. A model trained with KL has almost no incentive to keep mass out of the far
tails; an evaluation by energy score punishes exactly that. This is the second reason,
alongside the batch argument of Section~\ref{sec:tails}, why training does not correct the
tails, and it is the mechanism we propose for the $\tanh$ result of Section~\ref{sec:exp}.
It also predicts that training with the energy distance itself, which the paper's
Remark~3.5 allows, would penalise tail over-weighting directly. We have not tested this.

\paragraph{Flexible encoders make the family well-specified.} With biases, $\langle f(u),g(v)\rangle$
contains terms $c(u)+d(v)$. Sums $\sum_k f_k(u)g_k(v)$ of products of network features are
dense in the continuous functions on compact sets, by Stone--Weierstrass. Stuart \&
Wolf~\cite{stuartwolf} have made this precise for two-tower CLIP: with flexible enough
encoders and a learnable temperature, the model can approximate any joint distribution on
a compact domain to arbitrary accuracy in KL. Combined with Corollary~\ref{cor:agree}, this is
the first half of the thesis: once the encoders are flexible, every loss variant and every
divergence leads to the same forecast. Finding~2 is that convergence for the loss variants,
seen at finite data.

One caution about ``arbitrary accuracy''. Being close in KL does not make two distributions
close in energy distance: Proposition~\ref{prop:farmass} is a pair that is close in KL and
arbitrarily far apart in energy distance. KL-closeness controls the bulk; closeness in energy
distance also needs control of where the tail mass sits. So the divergences agree exactly at
the optimum, and approximately in the bulk, but an approximation that is good in one can be
poor in another in the tails. That is the second half of the thesis again.

\subsection{What happens when the family is too rigid: the Gaussian case}

The Gaussian case shows how the loss variants come apart when the family is not flexible,
and recovers P1, P3 and P4. Let $\mu$ be a centred Gaussian with covariance blocks
$C_{uu},C_{uv},C_{vv}$, joint precision $P$ and $D=\mathrm{blockdiag}(C_{uu},C_{vv})$. Then
\[
  \log r(u,v) = -\tfrac12 u^\top(P_{uu}-C_{uu}^{-1})u \;-\; u^\top P_{uv} v
                \;-\; \tfrac12 v^\top(P_{vv}-C_{vv}^{-1})v + \text{const}.
\]
There are three ingredients: a past--future interaction, which sets the forecast's average;
a future-only term, which sets its spread; and a past-only term. With linear encoders every
score is $s=-\tfrac12 u^\top Ru + u^\top Av - \tfrac12 v^\top Sv$, and the families differ in
which ingredients they can set:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
family & allowed $(R,A,S)$ & well-specified for \\
\midrule
dot product & $R=S=0$ & nothing (if $C_{uv}\neq0$) \\
\Ltwo{} & $\begin{psmallmatrix}R&-A\\-A^\top&S\end{psmallmatrix}=[G,-H]^\top[G,-H]\succeq0$ & one-way only \\
dot $+$ free quadratics & $R,S$ symmetric, $A$ free & every loss \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{P1.} The dot product has no future-only term, so its forecast's covariance is
  $C_{vv}$ whatever $A$ is: the spread is stuck at climatology.
\item \textbf{P4.} The joint loss also needs the past-only term, which the dot product lacks.
  On top of that, the joint normaliser is finite only if
  $\|C_{uu}^{1/2}AC_{vv}^{1/2}\|<1$, which is where the paper's shrinkage function $h$ comes
  from. Our numerical minimiser reproduces $h$ exactly.
\item \textbf{P2.} One-way \Ltwo{} is well-specified, because $S\succeq0$ can match the
  future-only term.
\end{itemize}

\begin{proposition}[Linear \Ltwo{} is too rigid for the two-way and joint losses]\label{prop:l2joint}
Under linear \Ltwo{}, every model joint covariance satisfies $(D^{-1}+Q)^{-1}\preceq D$, with
$Q=[G,-H]^\top[G,-H]\succeq0$. The data satisfy $C\not\preceq D$ whenever $C_{uv}\neq0$. So the two-way
and joint losses cannot reach zero. In words: an \Ltwo{} model can couple past and future
only by shrinking both marginals, and real data couple them without shrinking.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Scalar closed form]\label{prop:scalar}
For unit-variance scalars with correlation $\rho\in(0,1)$, the joint-\Ltwo{} optimum forecasts
$\mathcal N\big(\tfrac{\rho}{2-\rho}u,\ \tfrac{2(1-\rho)}{2-\rho}\big)$ against the truth
$\mathcal N(\rho u, 1-\rho^2)$. The average is shrunk and the forecast is over-confident. The
fraction of the information $I(u;v)$ it loses tends to $3/8$ as $\rho\to0$ and to $0$ as
$\rho\to1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lc|ccc|ccc|c@{}}
\toprule
& & \multicolumn{3}{c|}{dot product} & \multicolumn{3}{c|}{\Ltwo{}} & dot $+$ quad. \\
setting & $I(u;v)$ & 1-way & 2-way & joint & 1-way & 2-way & joint & all three \\
\midrule
$\rho=0.1$ & 0.005 & 0.005 & 0.005 & 0.005 & 0 & 0.294 & 0.333 & 0 \\
$\rho=0.3$ & 0.047 & 0.046 & 0.046 & 0.051 & 0 & 0.219 & 0.251 & 0 \\
$\rho=0.5$ & 0.144 & 0.131 & 0.131 & 0.157 & 0 & 0.148 & 0.170 & 0 \\
$\rho=0.7$ & 0.337 & 0.272 & 0.272 & 0.323 & 0 & 0.081 & 0.092 & 0 \\

Table~\ref{tab:miss} makes the picture quantitative. Two points stand out.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Each rigid family fails in its own regime.} The dot product's error is in the
  spread, which matters most when dependence is strong. \Ltwo{}'s two-way/joint error is the
  coupling-by-shrinking constraint, which matters most when dependence is weak. Our Gaussian
  process is strongly dependent ($I=1.63$ nats), so two-way and joint \Ltwo{} lose only 0.3\% of
  the information there. That is why Finding~1 showed all three \Ltwo{} variants at $+0.0\%$.
\item \textbf{Adding the missing ingredients removes every difference.} Giving the dot product
  free past-only and future-only quadratic terms makes all three losses exact (last column).
  This is the flexibility argument in miniature: complete the family and the loss stops
  mattering.
\end{itemize}

\section{Why the score still matters: its tails}\label{sec:tails}

Flexibility is about representing $\log r$ well where the data are. It says nothing about how
the score behaves far out, and that is where the second half of the thesis lives. Stuart \&
Wolf's extension of their approximation result to unbounded domains already needs the score
to be \emph{bounded} outside a compact set, with an error term that grows like $e^{B}$ in
that bound~\cite{stuartwolf}. We show what goes wrong without it.

\begin{definition}
A score $s$ is \emph{admissible} for $\mu$ if $Z(u)<\infty$ for $\mu_u$-almost every $u$. Then
the forecast $\nu_\vu$ exists and $J_\vu(s)$ is finite. Otherwise $J_\vu(s)=+\infty$ and there is
no model to learn.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Bounded above $\Rightarrow$ admissible for every distribution]\label{prop:bounded}
If $s\le c$, then $Z(u)\le e^c$ and $Z\le e^c$ for every $\mu$. So \Ltwo{} ($c=0$) and
normalised cosine ($c=\kappa$) are admissible for every data distribution, with any encoders.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[A score linear in the future needs light tails]\label{prop:linear}
If $s(u,v)=\langle a(u),Hv\rangle+b(u)$, then $Z(u)=e^{b(u)}M_{\mu_v}(H^\top a(u))$, where $M_{\mu_v}$ is the
moment generating function of the futures. For a multivariate Student-$t$ (any degrees of
freedom), $M_{\mu_v}(w)=\infty$ for every $w\ne0$. So the linear dot product is admissible only
if it ignores the past: every informative parameter has infinite loss.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[ReLU networks do not rescue the dot product]\label{prop:relu}
Let $g$ be a ReLU network and $g_\infty$ the same network with its biases removed. If
$\langle f(u),g_\infty(v_0)\rangle>0$ for some direction $v_0$ and the futures are elliptical
Student-$t$, then $Z(u)=\infty$. So the dot product is admissible at $u$ only if its score never
grows in any direction of the future, and nothing in training enforces that. A bounded output
layer on $g$ (such as $\tanh$) makes the score admissible, by Proposition~\ref{prop:bounded},
without normalising anything. Section~\ref{sec:exp} shows this is not enough in practice.
\end{proposition}

Admissibility is not a technicality. It is exactly the condition for the bank forecast to
work.

\begin{proposition}[Admissible $\Leftrightarrow$ the bank forecast converges]\label{prop:snis}
Fix $u$ and draw the bank i.i.d.\ from $\mu_v$. If $Z(u)<\infty$, then $\nu^N_\vu\to\nu_\vu$ weakly,
almost surely, and $\mathrm{ESS}/N\to1/\E_{\mu_v}[(d\nu_\vu/d\mu_v)^2]$ when that moment is finite.
If $Z(u)=\infty$, the normaliser $\frac1N\sum_j e^{s(u,v_j)}$ diverges and there is nothing to
converge to.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Collapse]\label{prop:collapse}
If the scores $X_j=s(u,v_j)$ of the bank futures have a regularly varying right tail,
$P(X>x)=x^{-\alpha}L(x)$, then $\max_jw_j\to1$ in probability as $N\to\infty$. This covers the linear
dot product on Student-$t$ futures, with $\alpha$ equal to the degrees of freedom.
\end{proposition}

So the thesis's two outcomes are, precisely: with a bounded score the bank forecast
\emph{converges to the model} as the bank grows; with an unbounded score on heavy tails it
\emph{collapses onto the single most extreme future}, and a bigger bank only supplies more
extreme futures to collapse onto. Table~\ref{tab:collapse} checks this for fixed scores, with
no training.

\begin{proposition}[Bounded on both sides: guaranteed bank, capped sharpness]\label{prop:twosided}
If $|s|\le\kappa$ (normalised cosine), then $e^{-2\kappa}\le d\nu_\vu/d\mu_v\le e^{2\kappa}$. Hence
$\KL(\nu_\vu\|\mu_v)\le2\kappa$ and $\mathrm{ESS}/N\ge e^{-2\kappa}$ in the limit, uniformly in $u$. The
price is that no forecast can be more than $e^{2\kappa}$ times as concentrated as climatology, or
carry more than $2\kappa$ nats of information.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{A subtlety: why training does not avoid the problem.} At the population level an
inadmissible parameter has infinite loss, so an exact minimiser would avoid it. But training
uses the empirical loss, which is always finite: a batch of 256 futures never contains the
extreme futures that make $Z(u)$ infinite. So training can settle on an informative
parameter whose score grows in the future, and the problem only shows up when the forecast
meets a large bank. For the linear dot product on Student-$t$ data, Proposition~\ref{prop:linear}
says every informative parameter is of this kind.

\section{Testing the tails directly}\label{sec:exp}

We ran two experiments, both on the heavy-tailed domain ($\nu=3$), with the Gaussian
process as a control.

\paragraph{Setup.} Each model is trained exactly as in Section~\ref{sec:evidence} (same data,
15k steps, batch 256), then frozen. Only the bank changes: the forecast is rebuilt from
held-out banks of $N=100$ up to $N=200{,}000$ futures, with up to 3 disjoint banks per size,
on the same 500 test pasts. We record the energy score above the perfect forecaster, the
largest bank weight, and, for collapsed forecasts (largest weight $>0.5$), how extreme the
winning future is: its percentile by size within the bank. 2 seeds on the heavy-tailed
domain, 1 for the control. The script is \texttt{test\_bed/analysis/bank\_sweep.py}.

\paragraph{Experiment 1: does a bigger bank help or hurt?} Theory
(Propositions~\ref{prop:snis}, \ref{prop:collapse}) predicts that bounded scores improve and
level off as the bank grows, and that the unbounded dot product turns around and gets worse.

The prediction holds. On heavy tails the unbounded dot product is best at a small bank and
then gets steadily worse: with MLP encoders from $+7\%$ at 3k futures to $+46\%$ at 200k, and
with linear encoders from $+33\%$ to over $+3000\%$. Every bounded score improves and levels
off. On the Gaussian control the same unbounded score also improves and levels off (at
$+5.7\%$ linear, matching the $+5.6\%$ of Finding~1), so the turnaround is caused by the tails,
not by the score alone. The collapse diagnostic shows the mechanism directly. With linear
encoders, 20\% of the dot product's forecasts at $N=200$k put more than half their weight on
one future, and that future is the most extreme in the bank (99.998th percentile by size,
seed 1).

\paragraph{Experiment 2: is a ceiling enough?} Proposition~\ref{prop:relu} says that a
$\tanh$ on the future encoder makes the dot product admissible without normalising
anything. The result is only a partial fix (Table~\ref{tab:banksweep}, second row of each
block):
\begin{itemize}
\item it is far better than the uncapped dot product ($+103\%$ against $+3358\%$ with linear
  encoders, $+7.5\%$ against $+46\%$ with MLPs at 200k);
\item but it still turns around: in one MLP seed it improves to $+1.2\%$, in the other it rises
  from $+8\%$ to $+14\%$; with linear encoders it rises from $+22\%$ to $+103\%$;
\item its forecasts rarely collapse onto one future, but the futures they favour are still
  the most extreme in the bank (99.99th percentile, linear, seed 1).
\end{itemize}
This does not contradict the theory. The ceiling makes the model exist, so the bank
forecast converges. But it converges to a model that over-weights extremes. A $\tanh$
score rises \emph{towards} its ceiling as the future becomes more extreme, so extreme futures
in the favoured direction receive the top score. A small bank contains few of them and
hides the problem, and a large bank exposes it.

\paragraph{What the two experiments say together.} The scores that work either
\emph{penalise} extreme futures (\Ltwo{}: the score falls as $g(v)$ moves away from $f(u)$) or
are \emph{indifferent} to how extreme a future is (normalised cosine depends only on the
direction of $g(v)$). It is not the height of the ceiling that matters: normalised cosine's
learned scale is about 72, so its ceiling is enormous, and it is the best model here. The
underlying reason is that training cannot correct the tails. A batch of 256 futures almost
never contains one from the far tail, so how the score treats extreme futures is set by the
architecture, not learned from data. A ceiling is \emph{necessary}: without one the model does
not exist. In practice the score must also not \emph{reward} extremity.

\section{Finite data: the loss matters again, for a different reason}\label{sec:finite}

The thesis is a statement about infinite data. With finite data the loss variant can matter
again, through over-fitting rather than through what it aims at. The clearest case is
weather, where one-way \Ltwo{} is the well-specified variant yet loses to joint:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
skill (2 seeds) & \Ltwo{} 1-way & \Ltwo{} 2-way & \Ltwo{} joint \\
\midrule
rain, linear (vs climatology) & $-0.106$ & $+0.011$ & $+0.012$ \\
temperature, MLP (vs persistence) & $+0.152$ & $+0.243$ & $+0.280$ \\
temperature, linear (vs persistence) & $+0.352$ & $+0.344$ & $+0.383$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The population argument bounds what joint and two-way \Ltwo{} can lose to rigidity: in the
scalar Gaussian, at most about $3/8$ (joint) or $1/3$ (two-way) of $I(u;v)$. On rain $I(u;v)$
is tiny (the best model beats climatology by 1\%), so that cost is negligible, and the
observed gaps must come from finite data. One-way \Ltwo{} has nothing that stops it from
becoming over-sharp, and on rain its forecasts collapsed (minimum ESS 1.6 with linear
encoders, median ESS 3 with MLPs). Two-way and joint \Ltwo{} do have such a check: by a Schur
complement, $S\succeq A^\top R^{+}A$, so a sharp forecast must come with a sharp backward
conditional, which the two-way loss penalises; the joint loss also scores the model's
marginals, which a collapsed forecast distorts. On this reading the joint loss's small bias
buys a regulariser against over-sharpening. It predicts that the one-way deficit shrinks as
training data grow, as it does on the plentiful synthetic data of Finding~2. This is a
hypothesis, not a result.

\section{Relation to the literature}\label{sec:lit}

A literature sweep (about fifteen targeted searches, October 2026) found the following
relatives. It was not exhaustive, so ``we did not find'' below means only that.

\paragraph{What is known, and what we build on.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The probabilistic framework} is that of Baptista, Stuart \& Tran~\cite{bst}.
  Stuart \& Wolf~\cite{stuartwolf} take the same population-level view and prove that
  two-tower CLIP is a universal approximator in KL on compact domains. Their extension to
  unbounded domains requires the score to be bounded outside a compact set, with an error
  term that grows like $e^{B}$ in that bound. They also bound the conditional KLs by the joint
  KL, but do not claim that the loss variants share minimisers. Our first half (the loss stops
  mattering) is their expressivity result combined with Proposition~\ref{prop:target}. Our
  second half explains why their boundedness condition is not just a convenience of the
  proof.
\item \textbf{The optimal score is the log density ratio.} That the InfoNCE optimum is the
  pointwise mutual information plus a function of the conditioning variable is standard
  (CPC~\cite{cpc}; Poole et al.~\cite{poole}). For CLIP's symmetric loss, Uesaka et
  al.~\cite{uesaka} show that the optimal similarity is the pointwise mutual information and
  argue that inner products of finite-dimensional embeddings may fail to reach it. They
  propose a richer similarity. That is the closest relative of our misspecification view, but
  it concerns a single loss, not the agreement or disagreement between variants.
\item \textbf{Sampling by reweighting candidates.} Sordoni et al.~\cite{sordoni} note that
  resampling candidates with softmax-normalised critic scores approximates $p(y\mid x)$, and
  that it is exact as the candidate pool grows. That is our bank forecast. They do not study
  when the limit fails to exist.
\item \textbf{Bounded critics and normalisation.} In mutual-information estimation, Song \&
  Ermon~\cite{songermon} clip the critic to tame the variance of partition-function
  estimates. In representation learning, normalised embeddings are standard because they
  remove the scale degree of freedom that lets the softmax become arbitrarily
  sharp~\cite{simclr,wangisola}. Negative squared Euclidean distance logits (our \Ltwo{}) are
  used in practice and match or beat cosine CLIP~\cite{euclip}. None of these ties
  boundedness to the \emph{existence} of the model or to the behaviour of a reweighted bank.
\item \textbf{Importance-sampling degeneracy.} Weight collapse is classical: in high
  dimensions it is studied by Bengtsson, Bickel \& Li~\cite{bbl}, heavy-tailed weights by
  Pareto-smoothed importance sampling~\cite{psis}, and the required sample size
  $\approx e^{\KL}$ by Chatterjee \& Diaconis~\cite{cd}. That an exponential tilt $e^{\langle a,v\rangle}$
  needs a finite moment generating function is textbook. Propositions~\ref{prop:snis}
  and~\ref{prop:collapse} apply these facts to contrastive scores; we claim no new probability.
\item \textbf{Analog forecasting.} Forecasting by weighting historical futures is the analog
  ensemble of weather forecasting~\cite{anen} and kernel analog forecasting~\cite{kaf}. A
  contrastive bank forecast is an analog forecast whose kernel, $e^{s(u,v)}$, is learned.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What we did not find.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The statement that the one-way, two-way and joint losses, under any divergence, agree
  exactly when the score family can represent $\log r$, with the one-way loss needing strictly
  less (Proposition~\ref{prop:target}, Corollary~\ref{cor:agree}). The divergence part is a
  short consequence of the definitions; its interest is in what it rules out.
\item That KL training is blind to how far misplaced forecast mass goes while the energy
  score is not, as an explanation of why bounded but extremity-rewarding scores fail
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:farmass}).
\item That linear \Ltwo{} is too rigid for the two-way and joint losses on any correlated
  Gaussian, with the closed form and the $3/8$ limit (Propositions~\ref{prop:l2joint},
  \ref{prop:scalar}).
\item That a score growing in the future has no valid model on heavy-tailed data even with
  ReLU encoders, and that its bank forecast then collapses and gets worse as the bank grows
  (Propositions~\ref{prop:linear}--\ref{prop:collapse}, Section~\ref{sec:exp}).
\item The thesis itself: with flexible encoders the choice of loss and of divergence drops
  out, and the choice of score survives only through its tails.
\end{itemize}

%======================================================================
\section{Scope and caveats}

\begin{itemize}
\item Sections~\ref{sec:loss} and~\ref{sec:tails} are population-level statements (infinite
  data, infinite negatives). The finite-sample statements are Propositions~\ref{prop:snis}
  and~\ref{prop:collapse}, the experiments of Section~\ref{sec:exp}, and the hypothesis of
  Section~\ref{sec:finite}.
\item The information losses in Table~\ref{tab:miss} are KL divergences; the experiments use
  the energy score, which is less sensitive to spread errors. The two should agree in sign,
  not in size.
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:relu} is proved for ReLU networks. Our MLPs use GELU, which grows
  like ReLU at infinity, so the same conclusion holds, but we have not written out that proof.
\item Rain is physically bounded, so its true $Z(u)$ is finite and Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse}
  does not apply literally. What applies is its finite-$N$ version: a tiny $\mathrm{ESS}/N$
  whenever the scores over the bank are heavy-tailed.
\item All experiments use 2 seeds, except the Gaussian control of Section~\ref{sec:exp}
  (1 seed). The collapse diagnostic was recorded for seed 1 only.
\item The ``does not reward extremity'' refinement of Section~\ref{sec:exp} is an empirical
  reading of two experiments on one domain. We have not stated or proved it as a theorem.
\item The divergence half of the thesis is proved but not tested: every model here was
  trained with KL. The test is to train with the energy-distance loss and compare. By
  Proposition~\ref{prop:farmass} we expect it to matter most for scores that reward
  extreme futures.
\item Untested so far: the weak-dependence Gaussian prediction of
  Proposition~\ref{prop:scalar} at finite data, the quadratic-augmented dot product
  (Table~\ref{tab:miss}, last column) in training, and the bank sweep on rain.
\end{itemize}

\section{Proofs}

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:target}.} Because $D(p,q)\ge0$ with equality only if $p=q$, each
loss is zero exactly when the distributions it compares are equal, whatever $D$ is. The
rest of the argument is therefore about equality of distributions and does not involve
$D$. (i) $J^D_\vu(s)=0$ iff $\nu_\vu(\cdot\mid u)=\mu_\vu(\cdot\mid u)$ for
a.e.\ $u$. Comparing densities with respect to $\mu_v$, that is $e^{s(u,v)}/Z(u)=r(u,v)$, i.e.\
$s=\log r+\log Z(u)$. Conversely, $s=\log r+c(u)$ gives $Z(u)=e^{c(u)}\int r(u,\cdot)\,d\mu_v=e^{c(u)}$ and
hence the true conditional. (ii) is symmetric. (iii) With $\lambda_u,\lambda_v>0$, $J^D_2=0$ iff (i) and
(ii) both hold, so $c(u)=c'(v)$ for $\mu_u\otimes\mu_v$-a.e.\ $(u,v)$. A function of $u$ alone that
equals a function of $v$ alone a.e.\ under a product measure is a.e.\ constant. For the joint
loss, $D(\mu,\nu)=0$ iff $\nu=\mu$ iff $e^{s}/Z=r$, i.e.\ $s=\log r+\log Z$. \qed

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:farmass}.} Since $p$ has no atom at $a$, $q_\varepsilon\ge(1-\varepsilon)p$ on
the support of $p$, so $dp/dq_\varepsilon\le1/(1-\varepsilon)$ $p$-a.e.\ and
$\KL(p\|q_\varepsilon)=\int\log(dp/dq_\varepsilon)\,dp\le-\log(1-\varepsilon)$. For the energy distance, write
$m=\E|X-X'|$ and $A=\E|X-a|$. With $Y\sim q_\varepsilon$, $\E|X-Y|=(1-\varepsilon)m+\varepsilon A$ and
$\E|Y-Y'|=(1-\varepsilon)^2m+2\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)A$. Substituting,
$D_E=2(1-\varepsilon)m+2\varepsilon A-m-(1-\varepsilon)^2m-2\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)A=\varepsilon^2(2A-m)$, and
$A\ge|a|-\E|X|\to\infty$. \qed

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:l2joint}.} The model joint has density proportional to
$e^{s}\,\mathcal N(0,D)$; with $s=-\frac12z^\top Qz$, $z=(u,v)$, that is
$\mathcal N(0,(D^{-1}+Q)^{-1})$. Since $D^{-1}+Q\succeq D^{-1}$, inverting reverses the order, so
$(D^{-1}+Q)^{-1}\preceq D$. On the data side,
$D-C=\begin{psmallmatrix}0&-C_{uv}\\-C_{uv}^\top&0\end{psmallmatrix}$ has eigenvalues
$\pm\sigma_i(C_{uv})$, so $C\preceq D$ forces $C_{uv}=0$. Zero two-way or joint loss requires the
model joint to equal $\mu$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:target}(iii)), which is impossible when
$C_{uv}\ne0$. The inequality $S\succeq A^\top R^+A$ is the generalised Schur criterion for
$\begin{psmallmatrix}R&-A\\-A^\top&S\end{psmallmatrix}\succeq0$. \qed

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:scalar}.} With $s=-\frac12(gu-hv)^2$ and unit variances,
$Q=\begin{psmallmatrix}g^2&-gh\\-gh&h^2\end{psmallmatrix}$ and, up to constants,
$2J_{\rm joint}=g^2+h^2-2gh\rho-\log(1+g^2+h^2)$. For fixed $t=g^2+h^2$ the cross term is minimised
at $g=h$, leaving $t(1-\rho)-\log(1+t)$, minimised at $t=\rho/(1-\rho)$. The forecast then has
precision $1+t/2=(2-\rho)/(2(1-\rho))$ and mean coefficient $\rho/(2-\rho)$. Expanding for small $\rho$
gives $J_\vu=\frac3{16}\rho^2+O(\rho^3)$ against $I(u;v)=\frac12\rho^2+O(\rho^4)$, so the ratio tends to
$3/8$. At $\rho=0.5$ the closed form gives $0.1699$, matching Table~\ref{tab:miss}. \qed

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:bounded}.} $Z(u)=\int e^{s}\,d\mu_v\le e^c$, and likewise for $Z$. For
\Ltwo{}, $-\frac\kappa2|f-g|^2\le0$; for normalised cosine, $|\langle\bar f,\bar g\rangle|\le1$ by
Cauchy--Schwarz. \qed

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:linear}.} $Z(u)=e^{b(u)}\E\exp\langle H^\top a(u),v\rangle$ by definition. A
multivariate $t$ is elliptical, so for $w\ne0$ the projection $\langle w,v\rangle$ is a scaled
univariate $t$, whose density decays polynomially; hence $\E e^{\langle w,v\rangle}=\infty$. \qed

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:relu}.} Write $g=W_L\sigma(\dots\sigma(W_1v+b_1)\dots)+b_L$ with
$\sigma=\mathrm{ReLU}$. ReLU is 1-Lipschitz and positively homogeneous, so by induction over layers
$|g(v)-g_\infty(v)|\le\sum_\ell\|b_\ell\|\prod_{k>\ell}\|W_k\|=:B<\infty$, and $g_\infty(tv)=tg_\infty(v)$ for $t\ge0$.
Let $\delta=\langle f(u),g_\infty(v_0)\rangle>0$. By continuity there is an open cone $K\ni v_0$ on which
$\langle f(u),g_\infty(v/|v|)\rangle\ge\delta'>0$, so on $K$, $s(u,v)\ge\kappa(\delta'|v|-|f(u)|B)$. An elliptical
$t$ gives every open cone a polynomial tail, $\mu_v(K\cap\{|v|>t\})\asymp t^{-\nu}$, so
$\int_K e^{\kappa\delta'|v|}\,d\mu_v=\infty$. \qed

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:snis}.} For bounded continuous $\varphi$, the strong law gives
$\frac1N\sum_j e^{s(u,v_j)}\varphi(v_j)\to\int e^{s}\varphi\,d\mu_v$ and $\frac1N\sum_je^{s(u,v_j)}\to Z(u)$ almost
surely, since both integrands are integrable when $Z(u)<\infty$. The ratio converges to
$\int\varphi\,d\nu_\vu$; a countable convergence-determining class gives weak convergence. With
$\rho=e^{s}/Z(u)$, $N\sum_jw_j^2=\frac{\frac1N\sum\rho(v_j)^2}{(\frac1N\sum\rho(v_j))^2}\to\E_{\mu_v}[\rho^2]$. If
$Z(u)=\infty$, the strong law for nonnegative variables gives $\frac1N\sum_je^{s(u,v_j)}\to\infty$. \qed

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse}.} Let $X_{(1)}\ge X_{(2)}$ be the two largest scores and
$b_N$ the $1-1/N$ quantile of $X$, so $b_N=N^{1/\alpha}\ell(N)$ with $\ell$ slowly varying. By the
extreme-value limit for regularly varying tails, $(X_{(1)},X_{(2)})/b_N$ converges in distribution
to $(\Gamma_1^{-1/\alpha},\Gamma_2^{-1/\alpha})$, the first two points of a Poisson process, which are a.s.\
distinct. So for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $c>0$ with $P(X_{(1)}-X_{(2)}\ge cb_N)\ge1-\varepsilon$ for
large $N$, and on that event $1-\max_jw_j\le\sum_{j\ne(1)}e^{X_j-X_{(1)}}\le Ne^{-cb_N}\to0$, because
$b_N$ grows polynomially. \qed

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:twosided}.} $|s|\le\kappa$ gives $Z(u)\in[e^{-\kappa},e^\kappa]$, so
$d\nu_\vu/d\mu_v=e^{s}/Z(u)\in[e^{-2\kappa},e^{2\kappa}]$. Then $\KL(\nu_\vu\|\mu_v)=\E_\nu\log(d\nu/d\mu_v)\le2\kappa$ and
$\E_{\mu_v}[(d\nu/d\mu_v)^2]=\E_\nu[d\nu/d\mu_v]\le e^{2\kappa}$; the ESS limit follows from
Proposition~\ref{prop:snis}. \qed

\texttt{test\_bed/theory/misspecification.py}; the experiments of Section~\ref{sec:exp} by
\texttt{test\_bed/analysis/bank\_sweep.py}.
